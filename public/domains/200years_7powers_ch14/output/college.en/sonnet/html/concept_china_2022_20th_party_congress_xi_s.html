At the 20th National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party in October 2022, Xi Jinping was confirmed for a third five-year term as General Secretary, the party's top position. Since 1982, Chinese political norms — established under Deng Xiaoping after the upheavals of Mao Zedong's decades-long rule — had held that top leaders serve two terms, roughly ten years, before handing power to a successor. Xi's third term broke that norm. It followed a 2018 constitutional amendment that removed presidential term limits, and it placed Xi alongside Mao as one of only two leaders in the People's Republic's history to hold power beyond a decade.

The Congress also revealed the mechanics of how this consolidation was executed. Xi filled the Politburo Standing Committee, the party's most powerful decision-making body, with close allies rather than rivals or potential successors, departing from the collective-leadership model that had balanced competing factions for decades. A widely circulated moment captured this shift symbolically: former leader Hu Jintao, seen as representing an older consensus-based faction, was escorted out of the closing session on live television, an event state media described as a health issue but that many analysts read as a signal of political marginalization.

To understand why this mattered beyond Chinese domestic politics, apply a simple framework used across political science: distinguish institutional norms (informal but binding expectations, like "two terms and out") from constitutional rules (formally codified limits). Term limits function as a self-binding commitment device — a way for a system to constrain future leaders, including successful ones, in exchange for predictable, non-violent transitions of power. When Xi's government amended the constitution in 2018 and then exercised that removed limit in 2022, it converted an informal norm into a formally eliminated rule, a stronger and more durable form of change than merely ignoring a norm once.

The practical implications compound over time: personalist rule concentrates decision-making in one leader, which can accelerate policy execution but also removes institutional checks on error-correction, since no internal rival or scheduled succession forces reconsideration of the leader's course, in areas ranging from economic policy to the COVID-19 response discussed in the preceding section.
