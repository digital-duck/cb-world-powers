China 1966 Cultural Revolution

The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) is Mao Zedong's campaign to reassert ideological control and eliminate rivals within the Communist Party by mobilizing millions of youth against "capitalist" and "traditional" elements in Chinese society. Organizationally, it is a top-down political mobilization: Mao and allies (notably the "Gang of Four") issued directives through party media and the newly formed Red Guards — student paramilitary groups authorized to identify, denounce, and punish anyone deemed insufficiently revolutionary. Unlike a bureaucratic reform, this campaign operated by suspending normal institutional checks — schools closed, courts were bypassed, and local officials could be overthrown by mob action — which is precisely what allowed it to spread so fast and cause such deep damage.

A representative episode: in 1966, Red Guards in Beijing targeted teachers, intellectuals, and "class enemies," subjecting them to public humiliation sessions called "struggle sessions," destroying religious sites and historical artifacts as relics of the "Four Olds" (old customs, culture, habits, ideas), and in many cases driving victims to suicide or death by violence. The campaign then radiated outward: millions of urban youth were later sent to the countryside ("Down to the Countryside Movement") for reeducation through labor, disrupting both education and agricultural production simultaneously.

When analyzing this concept, the useful problem-solving skill is tracing cause-and-consequence chains across political, social, and economic domains rather than treating it as an isolated event. Ask: what structural conditions from the Great Leap Forward's failures made Mao vulnerable to being sidelined, and how did the Cultural Revolution function as his political recovery strategy? Then trace forward: how did the closure of universities and persecution of educated elites produce a "lost generation" with long-term skill deficits, and how did that deficit later shape Deng Xiaoping's post-1976 reform priorities? A strong analytical answer identifies at least three linked effects — political (purge of rivals like Liu Shaoqi), social (loss of trust in institutions, generational trauma), and economic (industrial and agricultural disruption, human capital loss) — and explains the mechanism connecting each to the next, rather than listing them as unrelated facts. This chain-tracing approach is the core skill for evaluating any mass political mobilization in modern history.
