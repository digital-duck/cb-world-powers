Decolonization Wave Pattern

Definition. A decolonization wave pattern describes how a colonial power's overseas territories become independent not at random intervals but in a compressed sequence, driven by a shared underlying shift rather than territory-by-territory coincidence. The UK's empire loses its major holdings largely between 1947 and 1962, and France's follows an almost identical timeline. When two separate empires — with different colonies, different colonial administrations, and different domestic politics — collapse on the same clock, the pattern itself becomes evidence about causation, even before you decide what caused it.

Worked example. Consider the UK: India and Pakistan (1947), Ghana (1957), Nigeria (1960), and a dozen others by 1962. France: Morocco and Tunisia (1956), most of West and Equatorial Africa (1960), Algeria (1962). Line these up on a single timeline and the clustering is unmistakable — both empires shed most of their territory within the same fifteen-year band, immediately following World War II. A single-empire view might attribute each independence to local resistance movements or specific colonial failures. But the two-empire comparison forces a different question: what changed globally, around 1945–1947, that would push both empires toward exit on a similar schedule?

Problem-solving application. When you see synchronized timing across independent systems, you're choosing between two explanatory strategies, and the choice matters for how you build your argument. Strategy one: shared common cause — a global structural shift (the U.S. and USSR both discouraging old-style colonialism, war-exhausted European treasuries unable to fund occupation, the UN Charter legitimizing self-determination) pushes both empires along parallel tracks independently. Strategy two: causal diffusion — one empire's exit (e.g., Britain leaving India in 1947) changes the incentives or provides a template that accelerates the other's exit (French planners watching Indochina and Algeria in that light). These aren't mutually exclusive, and good historical argument doesn't require picking one exclusively — it requires stating explicitly which mechanism you think is doing more work and what evidence would distinguish them. A useful test: if common cause dominates, the timing should also line up with a third, unrelated empire (e.g., Dutch withdrawal from Indonesia, 1949) without any direct UK–France interaction being necessary. If diffusion dominates, you'd expect closer tracking of specific triggering events — one empire's crisis point should visibly precede shifts in the other's policy debates, not just their withdrawal dates.
