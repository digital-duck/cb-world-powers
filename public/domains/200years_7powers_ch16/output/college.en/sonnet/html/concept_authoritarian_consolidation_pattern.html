Authoritarian Consolidation Pattern

Definition. The authoritarian consolidation pattern describes a trajectory in which a political system moves from constrained or rotating leadership toward extended, personalized rule — typically through the formal removal or circumvention of term limits, combined with the informal accumulation of authority over party, military, and information institutions. This is not simply "a leader stays in power a long time"; it is a structural shift in the rules and norms that previously bounded executive tenure, replaced by mechanisms (constitutional amendment, party statute revision, controlled succession planning) that make indefinite rule procedurally legal within the system's own logic.

Worked example. Two chains in the underlying event data — one rooted in post-Soviet Russia, one in one-party China — independently produce this pattern despite starting from different institutional baselines. Russia's chain moves from a multi-party, term-limited presidency (1990s) through a sequence of office-rotation maneuvers and a 2020 constitutional amendment resetting term counts, enabling continued tenure into the 2020s. China's chain moves from a post-Mao norm of collective leadership and two-term limits (institutionalized in the 1980s–1990s) to the 2018 removal of presidential term limits, again enabling tenure beyond the prior informal ceiling. The chains diverge in starting system (competitive-but-managed elections vs. single-party governance) yet converge on the same structural endpoint: term-limit extension concurrent with leadership consolidation, both dated to the 2010s–2020s window in the source data.

Problem-solving application. When you are given a chain of dated political events and asked to classify it, look for three co-occurring markers rather than any single event: (1) a formal rule change affecting tenure limits, (2) concentration of appointment or oversight power in the same office, and (3) a compressed succession pipeline (fewer independent candidates positioned to inherit power). A chain exhibiting only one marker — e.g., a single long-serving leader under an unchanged constitution — is better classified as "long incumbency," not full consolidation. Applying this three-marker test to the Russia and China chains confirms both satisfy all three concurrently, which is what justifies grouping them under one pattern despite their different starting systems — the value of a cross-chain pattern like this is precisely that it lets you predict which markers to search for in a third, unlabeled chain.
