Payoff

Pull back to 1945–2025 as a whole and a pattern emerges: every leadership transition among the seven powers tracks a prior transition in science. The space race set the terms of the US-Soviet contest, but it also built the computing and satellite infrastructure that outlived the Cold War itself. That infrastructure matured into the internet, which by the 1990s had picked a new winner — the US technology sector globalized faster than any manufacturing base could, while also producing the backlash (deindustrialized regions, populist politics) that reshaped domestic coalitions in the US, UK, and later fed anti-globalization currents in Europe. In parallel, the decoding of DNA — itself downstream of transistor-enabled sequencing machines — created a biomedical and agricultural industry that added a second axis of economic power, one where the US, and increasingly China, invested heavily. By the 2010s these two threads — computing and genomics — converged into a third: artificial intelligence, which depends on both the semiconductor lineage of the transistor and the data infrastructure of the internet, and which now looks, to policymakers in Washington and Beijing alike, like the decisive determinant of next-generation military and economic power.

The synthesis is the argument that these were never four separate stories. A single technological lineage — vacuum tube to transistor to integrated circuit to networked computer to machine-learning model — kept resurfacing as the leverage point at each transition. In 1957, that leverage point was rocketry; in 1991, it was networking protocols; in 2023, it is training compute and model architecture. Each time, the power that controlled the leverage point gained disproportionate influence over the others, which is exactly why the AI export-control regime the US imposed on China in 2022–2023 has historical precedent in COCOM-era semiconductor controls — the same mechanism, updated for a new technology.

This is the textbook's final claim: to explain the last two centuries of great-power competition, you must treat science as a causal actor, not a footnote. Compare this synthesis against a contemporary case — an AI export-control decision, a genomics policy debate, an infrastructure investment package — and ask which historical leverage point it most resembles. That comparison is the payoff of the entire book.
