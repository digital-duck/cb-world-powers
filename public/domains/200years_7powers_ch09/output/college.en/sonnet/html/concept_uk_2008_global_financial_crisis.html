Uk 2008 Global Financial Crisis

The 2008 Global Financial Crisis began as a private-sector failure — collapsing US mortgage securities that spread losses through interconnected banks worldwide — but in the UK it quickly became a public-sector emergency. When Northern Rock suffered a bank run in September 2007 and then, in 2008, Royal Bank of Scotland and Lloyds Banking Group faced insolvency, the government intervened with direct capital injections, asset guarantees, and partial nationalization. RBS ended up roughly 84% government-owned; Lloyds around 43%. The total exposure of UK taxpayers to support schemes (loans, guarantees, and equity combined) reached several hundred billion pounds, though the eventual net cost after asset sales was far smaller.

Worked example — reading the balance-sheet logic. A bank fails when liabilities (deposits, debts) exceed the market value of its assets (loans, mortgage-backed securities). If a bank holds $A$ in assets and $L$ in liabilities, its equity is $E = A - L$. When the market suddenly reprices mortgage assets downward, $A$ falls; if it falls enough that $E$ turns negative, the bank is technically insolvent even though it may still be receiving deposits. Government "recapitalization" is simply raising $A$ (via cash injection in exchange for shares) so that $E$ returns to a positive, regulator-satisfying level. This is why the intervention took the form of buying bank shares rather than just lending cash — a loan increases $L$ along with $A$, leaving $E$ unchanged.

Problem-solving application. The crisis illustrates a policy dilemma students should be able to reason through, not just recall: bailing out banks was justified by systemic-risk logic (letting large, interconnected banks fail risks a cascading credit freeze that damages the wider economy, distinct from an individual firm's failure), but it also produced a legitimacy problem — private losses were socialized onto public balance sheets. This is causally linked to the UK's prior fiscal position: the costs of financing the Iraq War and other commitments left less room to absorb bailout costs without a corresponding policy shift. The result was the subsequent austerity era: governments cut public spending to rebuild fiscal space, arguing that high deficits from crisis-era borrowing needed to be reduced. Evaluating austerity therefore requires weighing two competing models of recovery — fiscal consolidation to restore market confidence versus fiscal stimulus to sustain demand — a debate with no single correct answer, but one that turns directly on how one models the relationship between government debt, interest rates, and economic growth.
