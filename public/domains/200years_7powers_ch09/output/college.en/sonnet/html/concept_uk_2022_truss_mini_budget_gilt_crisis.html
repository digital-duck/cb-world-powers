Uk 2022 Truss Mini Budget Gilt Crisis

On September 23, 2022, Chancellor Kwasi Kwarteng, acting under new Prime Minister Liz Truss, delivered a "mini-budget" proposing roughly £45 billion in unfunded tax cuts — abolishing the top income tax rate, reversing a planned corporation tax rise, and cutting stamp duty — without accompanying spending cuts or an independent forecast from the Office for Budget Responsibility. The announcement broke with a fiscal norm that markets had come to expect: major tax changes should be paired with a credible plan showing how they would be financed. Investors interpreted the omission as a signal of fiscal recklessness at a moment when inflation was already high and the Bank of England was raising interest rates.

The market reaction was immediate and severe. UK government bonds ("gilts") sold off sharply, pushing yields up at a pace not seen in decades, while the pound fell to $1.03, its lowest level against the dollar on record. The gilt sell-off exposed a hidden vulnerability in the pension system: many UK defined-benefit pension funds used "liability-driven investment" (LDI) strategies that relied on gilts as collateral for derivative positions. As gilt prices fell, funds faced margin calls, forcing them to sell more gilts to raise cash — a self-reinforcing spiral that threatened to spread instability through the pension sector. The Bank of England intervened on September 28 with a temporary, targeted gilt-buying program to halt the spiral, explicitly separating this liquidity backstop from its ongoing inflation-fighting stance.

Applying this case requires distinguishing between fiscal policy and financial stability: the mini-budget was a fiscal shock, but its transmission into a full-blown crisis depended on structural leverage inside the pension system that most observers hadn't scrutinized. This illustrates a recurring pattern in financial crises — a policy shock finds and amplifies an existing fragility (here, LDI leverage) that was invisible until stressed. Truss reversed nearly all the measures, dismissed Kwarteng, and resigned on October 20, having served 49 days, the shortest tenure of any British prime minister — a consequence of losing market and party confidence simultaneously.
