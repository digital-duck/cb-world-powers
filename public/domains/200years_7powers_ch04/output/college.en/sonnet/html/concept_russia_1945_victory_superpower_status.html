Russia 1945 Victory Superpower Status

Victory over Germany transformed the Soviet Union from a nation fighting for survival into one of two global superpowers, but the transformation rested on catastrophic human cost and produced a settlement whose ambiguities shaped the next half-century of world politics.

By early 1945, the momentum built at Kursk had carried the Red Army across Poland and into Germany itself. Soviet forces reached Berlin in April, and after brutal urban combat, the city fell in early May, forcing Germany's unconditional surrender. The Soviet Union's role in defeating Nazi Germany was decisive by any material measure: it inflicted the large majority of German military casualties and absorbed the war's heaviest toll in return, with estimates of roughly 27 million Soviet dead — a figure encompassing soldiers, POWs, and civilians killed by combat, starvation, and occupation policy. This casualty ratio is central to understanding postwar Soviet behavior: the USSR emerged victorious but devastated, its western territories leveled, and its leadership determined that such an invasion — Germany had struck twice in three decades — could never happen again.

That determination shaped diplomacy at the February 1945 Yalta Conference, where Stalin met Franklin Roosevelt and Winston Churchill to plan the postwar order. The three leaders agreed on Germany's division into occupation zones, the founding of the United Nations, and Soviet entry into the Pacific war, but the more consequential outcome was implicit rather than written down: Soviet military presence across Eastern Europe, established by the same advance that had defeated Hitler, became the basis for postwar political control. Vague Yalta language promising "free elections" in liberated countries was interpreted very differently by each side — a divergence that would soon harden into Cold War division.

Apply this to a broader problem: explaining why "who liberates" and "who governs" are not the same question. Territory occupied by an army at war's end typically remains under that army's influence during the transition to peace, regardless of formal agreements. This is why Eastern Europe fell under Soviet influence while Western Europe, liberated primarily by American and British forces, developed under U.S.-aligned governments — the occupation map at V-E Day, more than any treaty clause, predicted the shape of Europe's coming division into two blocs.
