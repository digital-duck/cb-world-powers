Russia 1918 Russian Civil War

The Bolsheviks' seizure of power in October 1917 did not settle Russia's future — it triggered a war to determine whether that power would survive. From 1918 to 1922, the Red Army (the Bolshevik-led military, commanded largely by Leon Trotsky) fought a loose coalition known as the White Army: monarchists hoping to restore the tsar, liberal constitutionalists who wanted a democratic republic, and former tsarist generals, none of whom agreed on what should replace Bolshevik rule beyond opposing it. Foreign powers — Britain, France, the United States, and Japan among them — sent troops and supplies to aid the Whites, alarmed by Bolshevik promises to spread revolution and by Russia's exit from World War I, which had abandoned the Allied eastern front.

The Reds held a decisive strategic advantage: they controlled the densely populated, industrially connected core of Russia — Moscow, Petrograd, and the rail network linking them — while the Whites were scattered around the periphery in Siberia, the south, and the Baltic, unable to coordinate their offensives. Trotsky's centralized command turned the old imperial officer corps and industrial base into a functioning war machine, while "War Communism" — grain requisitioning, nationalized industry, forced labor — kept the front supplied at devastating cost to the civilian population. The Whites, by contrast, never unified ideologically or militarily, and their reliance on foreign troops let Bolshevik propaganda brand them as agents of foreign interference rather than as a genuine alternative.

Applying this case requires distinguishing military outcome from human cost. The Reds won: by 1922, Bolshevik control extended over nearly all former imperial territory, and the war's end allowed the formal founding of the Soviet Union that same year. But victory came atop a shattered country — a combination of combat deaths, the deliberate famine tactics of War Communism, and disease epidemics killed an estimated 7 to 12 million people, dwarfing the toll of the 1917 revolution itself. When analyzing this period, note the causal chain it establishes for what follows: the wartime machinery of forced requisitioning and one-party control, built as emergency measures, became the institutional template the Soviet state normalized rather than dismantled once the fighting stopped.
