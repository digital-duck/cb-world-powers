Russia 1928 First Five Year Plan

In 1928, Joseph Stalin terminated the New Economic Policy (NEP), the mixed-market compromise Lenin had introduced in 1921 to permit small-scale private trade and peasant agriculture alongside state control of "the commanding heights" of industry. In its place, the Soviet government launched the First Five-Year Plan: a centrally administered program setting fixed production targets for coal, steel, electricity, and machinery, all owned and directed by the state. The change rested on a strategic judgment: the USSR was economically and militarily behind the industrialized West, and market mechanisms would take too long to close that gap. Central planning promised speed by concentrating capital in heavy industry rather than letting consumer demand or private profit decide investment.

To fund this industrial buildup, the state needed grain — both to feed growing urban labor forces and to export for hard currency to buy foreign machinery. Individual peasant farms were judged too small and inefficient to supply this reliably, so the government forced their consolidation into kolkhozes (collective farms) and sovkhozes (state farms), a policy known as collectivization. This was not persuasion but a coercive campaign: peasants who resisted, particularly wealthier farmers labeled "kulaks," faced confiscation of land and livestock, deportation, or worse. Millions of peasants responded by slaughtering their own animals and destroying grain rather than surrendering it, causing agricultural output to collapse and contributing directly to the catastrophic famines of the early 1930s, including the Holodomor in Ukraine.

The problem-solving lesson here is about tracing causal chains in a planned economy: a policy goal (rapid industrial catch-up) generates an input requirement (capital and grain), which dictates an organizational method (collectivization), which in turn produces unintended but foreseeable consequences (resistance, output collapse, famine). When analyzing any centrally planned system, ask three linked questions — what output is being maximized, what resource is being extracted to pay for it, and who bears the extraction cost — since the answer to the third question typically explains where resistance and human cost concentrate. This causal structure recurs whenever a state prioritizes aggregate output targets over the incentives and welfare of the individual producers it depends on.
