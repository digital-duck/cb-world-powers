Russia 1922 Formation Ussr

By late 1922, four years of civil war and famine had exhausted Soviet Russia, but the Bolsheviks now controlled most of the former Russian Empire's territory through a patchwork of nominally independent republics: the Russian SFSR, Ukraine, Belarus, and the Transcaucasian Federation (Armenia, Georgia, Azerbaijan). On December 30, 1922, delegates from these republics signed the Treaty on the Creation of the USSR, formally establishing the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics — a federal state built on the principle that each republic retained (on paper) the right to secede and manage its own cultural affairs, while the Communist Party centralized economic and military policy.

Worked example. Consider the drafting debate between Lenin and Stalin, since it illustrates why the USSR's structure was a deliberate compromise rather than a foregone conclusion. Stalin, as Commissar of Nationalities, proposed "autonomization": the non-Russian republics would simply join the RSFSR as autonomous regions, subordinate to Moscow. Lenin rejected this, fearing it would look like — and function as — Russian chauvinism dressed up as internationalism, alienating the very minority nationalities the revolution claimed to liberate. Lenin's alternative, adopted at the treaty signing, created a new union of formally equal republics, each with its own government, flag, and (in theory) sovereignty, joined voluntarily under a shared federal structure. This distinction mattered enormously in practice: it gave Soviet nationality policy a veneer of consent and self-determination that later regimes, and later secessionist movements in 1991, could invoke.

Problem-solving application. When analyzing why the USSR's 1922 founding structure later enabled its 1991 dissolution along exactly the same republic borders, trace the causal chain: Lenin's federal design assigned each republic its own administrative apparatus and defined territorial boundary — infrastructure that, once nationalist movements revived under Gorbachev's glasnost, provided ready-made institutional and legal frameworks for independence claims. Contrast this with a counterfactual: had Stalin's autonomization plan prevailed, republics would have lacked distinct governing institutions, making a 1991-style breakup along the same lines far less likely. This exercise — asking what institutional feature must exist for a later historical event to unfold as it did — is a general technique for connecting founding decisions to long-term political consequences.
