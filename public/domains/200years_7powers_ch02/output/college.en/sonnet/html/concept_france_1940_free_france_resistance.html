France 1940 Free France Resistance

When Marshal Pétain's government signed an armistice with Nazi Germany in June 1940, most of the French political establishment accepted defeat. General Charles de Gaulle, a junior minister with no significant political base, refused. From London, he broadcast the Appeal of 18 June, urging French citizens to continue fighting, and began organizing the Free French Forces (Forces Françaises Libres) — an army-in-exile with no territory, treasury, or formal legitimacy at its founding. This is the core problem Free France had to solve: how does a government-in-exile generate legitimacy and military capability from nothing but a claim?

De Gaulle's answer combined three levers. First, symbolic legitimacy: he positioned himself as the embodiment of the "true" French state, rejecting the Vichy regime's legal claim to represent France. Second, territorial legitimacy: Free France gradually accumulated administrative control over French colonial territories in Equatorial Africa and elsewhere, giving it resources, manpower, and a governable base — something a purely symbolic movement in London lacked. Third, coalition legitimacy: alliance with Britain, and later the United States and the Soviet Union, gave Free France diplomatic standing even though Roosevelt initially distrusted de Gaulle and preferred dealing with Vichy-adjacent figures.

Inside occupied France, a separate but connected struggle unfolded: the Resistance. This was not one organization but a patchwork of networks — communist cells, Gaullist sympathizers, socialists, and independent actors — engaged in sabotage (cutting rail lines, disrupting German supply movements), intelligence gathering (feeding Allied planners information on troop positions and fortifications), and eventually armed insurgency. Jean Moulin's mission, sent by de Gaulle in 1942, illustrates the coordination problem directly: dozens of factional Resistance groups had incompatible politics and no unified command. Moulin's task was to fuse them into the National Council of the Resistance, trading local autonomy for a single chain of authority that could receive Allied arms drops and align its actions with the invasion timetable.

Apply this lesson by analyzing any resistance or insurgent movement you study: identify what makes its claim to legitimacy credible (symbolic, territorial, or coalitional), and separately assess whether its constituent factions are unified or fragmented — the latter question predicts whether external allies can coordinate support effectively, as the Allies eventually did with the French Resistance ahead of D-Day.
