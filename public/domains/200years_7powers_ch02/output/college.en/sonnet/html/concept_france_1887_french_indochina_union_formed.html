France 1887 French Indochina Union Formed

Definition. In 1887, France merged its colonial holdings in Southeast Asia—Cochinchina (a direct colony since 1862), the protectorates of Annam and Tonkin (secured after the Sino-French War), and the Cambodian protectorate—into a single administrative structure called the Indochinese Union (Union Indochinoise). Laos was added in 1893 after France pressured Siam to cede territory east of the Mekong. A Governor-General, based in Hanoi, oversaw the entire federation, coordinating finance, infrastructure, and security across territories that retained different legal statuses: Cochinchina was ruled directly as a colony, while Annam, Tonkin, Cambodia, and Laos remained nominally protectorates with local monarchs kept as figureheads.

Worked example. Consider why France chose a federal structure rather than annexing everything outright as one colony. Direct annexation would have required dismantling existing monarchies and administrative systems, an expensive and destabilizing process. Instead, the protectorate model let France retain local rulers (the Vietnamese emperor, the Cambodian king) as symbolic authorities while French résidents supérieurs controlled actual policy—taxation, justice, and foreign affairs. This hybrid approach reduced administrative cost and blunted immediate resistance, since local elites who cooperated retained status and privilege, even as sovereignty was hollowed out beneath them.

Problem-solving application. When analyzing any colonial or federal administrative system, ask three diagnostic questions: (1) What is the legal status of each component territory—colony, protectorate, or mandate—and what powers does that status formally reserve to local authorities versus the metropole? (2) Where does fiscal and military authority actually sit, regardless of formal titles? (3) What economic or strategic logic motivated centralization—here, unifying customs, currency (the piastre), rail and road networks, and the lucrative opium and salt monopolies simplified extraction of resources like rice, rubber, and minerals for export to France. Applying this framework to French Indochina reveals that the union was less about administrative tidiness than about creating an integrated economic zone that could be governed cheaply through indirect rule while maximizing revenue extraction—a pattern worth comparing to other federated colonial systems, such as British India's mix of directly ruled provinces and princely states, to see how similar cost-benefit calculations shaped different empires' administrative choices.
