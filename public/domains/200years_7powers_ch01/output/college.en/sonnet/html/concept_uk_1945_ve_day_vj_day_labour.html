VE Day (May 8, 1945) marked Germany's unconditional surrender; VJ Day (August 15/September 2, 1945) marked Japan's, ending the Second World War. In Britain the celebration was immediately followed by a political shock: the July 1945 general election, held in the war's final weeks, produced a landslide for the Labour Party under Clement Attlee, which won 393 seats to the Conservatives' 213 — a decisive rejection of Winston Churchill despite his wartime popularity. Voters, remembering mass unemployment and inadequate housing after WWI, chose the party promising reconstruction over the one associated with pre-war austerity.

Worked example. The scale of the shift is easier to grasp through the numbers. Labour's parliamentary majority was 146 seats — enough to govern without coalition partners, unlike the fragile arrangements of the interwar period. This majority was the mechanism that let Attlee's government act quickly: between 1945 and 1948, Parliament passed the National Insurance Act, the National Health Service Act, and nationalized industries including coal, rail, and steel. Compare this to a government with only a slim majority (say, 10–15 seats): such a majority is vulnerable to backbench rebellions and by-election losses, making sweeping, unpopular-with-some legislation politically risky to pursue. A 146-seat cushion meant Labour could absorb dissent and still pass contested bills — this is why the size of an electoral mandate, not just the fact of winning, matters for what a government can actually achieve in office.

Problem-solving application. When analyzing any parliamentary election result, ask two linked questions: (1) What underlying conditions — economic anxiety, war fatigue, unmet promises — explain the vote swing? (2) What does the resulting seat margin enable or constrain? Applying this framework to 1945: the "why" was widespread demand for social security after depression and war; the "what it enabled" was a majority large enough to build the welfare state (NHS, nationalization, expanded National Insurance) within a single Parliament. This two-step approach — diagnose the cause of a swing, then assess what the resulting majority permits — is a reusable tool for evaluating any election result's downstream policy consequences, not just this one.
