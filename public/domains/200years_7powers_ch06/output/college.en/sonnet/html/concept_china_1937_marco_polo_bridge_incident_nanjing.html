China 1937 Marco Polo Bridge Incident Nanjing

On the night of July 7, 1937, Japanese troops conducting exercises near the Marco Polo Bridge outside Beijing exchanged fire with Chinese soldiers of the 29th Army. A soldier went briefly missing; Japanese commanders demanded to search the nearby town of Wanping. Chinese officers refused, and within hours the skirmish escalated into artillery exchanges. Neither government had planned for a full war that month, but neither side would back down from what became a test of resolve, and by August, Japan had committed hundreds of thousands of troops to China, opening the Second Sino-Japanese War.

This incident illustrates a recurring dynamic in the outbreak of wars: a small, localized clash becomes a trigger point not because of its own military significance, but because it forces both governments to choose between de-escalation and reputational cost. Japan's military leadership, having already seized Manchuria in 1931, saw the incident as an opportunity to expand its foothold; Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist government, having just consolidated its position after the Long March and the Xi'an Incident, calculated that further retreat would fatally undermine its legitimacy. Both sides let the confrontation escalate rather than absorb the political cost of a compromise.

The war's early trajectory culminated in December 1937 with the fall of Nanjing, then the Nationalist capital. Japanese forces occupied the city and, over roughly six weeks, killed an estimated 200,000 to 300,000 civilians and disarmed soldiers through mass executions, and committed widespread sexual violence. The event is documented through Japanese military records, foreign diplomats and missionaries who remained in the city's international safety zone, and postwar tribunal testimony, though the exact death toll remains disputed between Chinese and some Japanese historians.

When analyzing this sequence, the useful comparative question is not "who fired first" but "why did de-escalation fail." Consider the incentive structure each government faced: Japan's field commanders operated with substantial autonomy from Tokyo, meaning local escalation could outrun diplomatic control — a pattern also visible in the 1931 Mukden Incident that triggered the invasion of Manchuria. Testing this generalization against other conflicts (the 1914 assassination in Sarajevo, for instance) helps clarify when a local incident functions as a genuine cause of war versus merely its occasion.
