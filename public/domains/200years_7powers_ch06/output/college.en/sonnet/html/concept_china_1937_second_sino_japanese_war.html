China 1937 Second Sino Japanese War

Definition. The Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945) was a full-scale conflict between the Republic of China and the Empire of Japan, sparked by the Marco Polo Bridge Incident near Beijing in July 1937 and escalating within months into the Nanjing Massacre, in which Japanese forces killed an estimated 200,000–300,000 civilians and prisoners of war in China's then-capital. The war forced China's rival factions — Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalists (Kuomintang) and Mao Zedong's Communists — into a Second United Front, a fragile wartime alliance built on shared survival rather than shared ideology.

Worked example. Consider the strategic logic of the united front as a case study in coalition behavior under asymmetric threat. Before 1937, the Nationalists and Communists had fought a bitter civil war for a decade. Japan's invasion changed the payoff structure: continued civil war risked total conquest by a third party, making cooperation the dominant strategy for both sides even though neither trusted the other. In practice this meant Communist forces (the Eighth Route Army) nominally operated under Nationalist command while Chiang's government relocated its capital westward to Chongqing and traded space for time, absorbing enormous territorial losses while preserving its army and (crucially) hosting Allied supply lines like the Burma Road once World War II broadened the conflict after 1941.

Problem-solving application. Historians use this case to explain why wartime alliances often unravel once the external threat recedes. Apply that logic forward: because the united front was a response to a specific threat rather than a resolution of the underlying conflict, ask what should happen to the alliance once Japan is defeated in 1945. The answer — renewed civil war between 1946 and 1949 — is not a separate, disconnected event but the predictable consequence of an alliance whose only shared interest was survival against Japan. When analyzing any coalition (wartime, political, corporate), a useful diagnostic question is: does this alliance rest on shared long-term goals, or only on a shared adversary? The Sino-Japanese War is the clearest instance in modern Chinese history where the second condition applied, and tracing what happens when the adversary disappears explains the civil war that follows almost mechanically.
