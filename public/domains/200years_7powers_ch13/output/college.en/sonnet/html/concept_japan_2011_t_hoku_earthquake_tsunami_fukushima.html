Japan 2011 T Hoku Earthquake Tsunami Fukushima

On March 11, 2011, a magnitude-9.0 undersea earthquake struck off Japan's northeastern coast — the most powerful ever recorded in Japan and the fourth-largest globally since modern seismographs began. The rupture along the Japan Trench displaced the seafloor by tens of meters, generating a tsunami that reached run-up heights over 40 meters in some coastal valleys. Waves overwhelmed seawalls built for smaller historical tsunamis and swept miles inland across flat coastal plains, destroying towns in Iwate, Miyagi, and Fukushima prefectures. Roughly 18,000 people died or went missing, the vast majority from drowning rather than the earthquake itself — a pattern consistent with most tsunami disasters, where the secondary wave, not the initial shaking, causes the bulk of casualties.

The tsunami's most consequential strike was on the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant. The earthquake triggered an automatic shutdown of the reactors, as designed, but the cooling systems needed to remove residual decay heat depended on backup diesel generators. The tsunami, exceeding the plant's protective seawall by several meters, flooded those generators and the electrical switchgear. Without power, operators lost the ability to circulate coolant; fuel rods overheated, their zirconium cladding reacted with steam to produce hydrogen, and hydrogen explosions breached containment in three reactor buildings. The result was the worst nuclear accident since Chernobyl, releasing radioactive material and forcing the evacuation of roughly 150,000 residents.

The disaster forced a direct reassessment of risk engineering: safety margins had been calibrated to historical tsunami records that underestimated the region's worst-case event, and a single-point failure — loss of all power — cascaded into meltdown despite multiple supposedly independent safety systems. In response, Japan shut down its entire nuclear fleet for inspection, later restarting only a fraction of reactors under stricter standards, and accelerated investment in liquefied natural gas and renewables to cover the shortfall. The episode reshaped nuclear regulation worldwide, prompting stress tests and backup-power redundancy reviews from the U.S. to the EU, and remains a central case study in how compounding low-probability events can defeat engineering assumptions built on incomplete historical data.
