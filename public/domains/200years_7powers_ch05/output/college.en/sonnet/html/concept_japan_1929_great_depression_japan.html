Japan's economy entered the Great Depression already strained: it had just returned to the gold standard in January 1930, an anti-inflationary move that made Japanese exports more expensive abroad at the worst possible moment. As global demand collapsed, Japan's two dominant exports—raw silk and cotton textiles—lost their American and European buyers almost overnight. Because roughly 40 percent of Japanese farm households supplemented their income by raising silkworms, the crash in silk prices (which fell by more than half between 1929 and 1931) struck the countryside directly. Rice prices fell alongside silk, so farmers earned less from both their staple crop and their cash crop simultaneously—a double blow with no cushion.

The human consequences were severe: tenant farmers defaulted on rents, families sold daughters into indentured labor or prostitution to survive, and rural suicide rates climbed. Unlike the urban unemployment crises familiar from the American Depression, Japan's crisis was disproportionately agrarian and regional, concentrated in the northeast (Tōhoku), where crop failures compounded the price collapse.

This economic catastrophe reshaped Japanese politics by discrediting the party-based parliamentary system that had governed since the 1920s. Ordinary citizens and, crucially, junior military officers—many themselves from farming families—came to see party politicians (seitō) and the zaibatsu conglomerates that funded them as corrupt, self-interested, and indifferent to rural suffering. This resentment fed directly into ultranationalist and militarist movements, which offered an alternative narrative: national renewal through military expansion, state-directed economic control, and the seizure of resource-rich territory abroad (soon realized in Manchuria in 1931).

Apply this reasoning to a comparative problem: contrast Japan's 1929–31 crisis with the U.S. Depression response. Where American New Dealers expanded domestic relief and regulation to preserve democratic capitalism, Japanese elites facing a similar legitimacy crisis increasingly ceded ground to military solutions. Ask what made the Japanese case diverge—consider the absence of a strong domestic reform coalition, the military's institutional autonomy from civilian oversight, and how the earlier U.S. Immigration Act of 1924 had already foreclosed emigration as an economic safety valve for excess rural population, intensifying domestic pressure. This comparison illustrates how identical economic shocks can produce very different political outcomes depending on existing institutional constraints.
