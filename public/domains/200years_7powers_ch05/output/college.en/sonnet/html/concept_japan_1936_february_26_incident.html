Japan 1936 February 26 Incident

Definition. The February 26 Incident (Ni Ni-Roku Jiken) was an attempted coup in Tokyo on February 26, 1936, carried out by roughly 1,400 troops under junior Army officers aligned with the radical "Imperial Way Faction" (Kōdōha). The rebels assassinated several senior government figures—including Finance Minister Takahashi Korekiyo and Interior Minister (former Home Minister) Saitō Makoto—and occupied central Tokyo for three days, demanding a "Shōwa Restoration" that would sweep away party politicians and financial elites in favor of direct military-backed imperial rule. Emperor Hirohito personally refused to legitimize the revolt, ordering it crushed; the rebellion collapsed without popular or wider military support, and its leaders were tried in secret and executed.

Worked example. Trace the causal chain: the 1933 Tanggu Truce had already shown that field commanders in Manchuria and North China could dictate terms to Tokyo with little civilian oversight, rewarding aggressive junior officers with prestige and precedent. That imbalance fed factional rivalry within the Army—the Kōdōha, who favored a Soviet-focused war built on spiritual mobilization, versus the rival Tōseiha ("Control Faction"), who favored a more bureaucratized, total-war economy targeting resource-rich Southeast Asia. The February 26 Incident was the Kōdōha's failed bid to seize power outright. Its failure, paradoxically, strengthened its rivals: the Tōseiha and its civilian allies used the crisis to purge Kōdōha sympathizers from the officer corps, then used the memory of the revolt as leverage over remaining civilian politicians, who now feared assassination if they resisted military demands.

Problem-solving application. This is a case where students should practice distinguishing an event's immediate effect from its structural effect. A common error is to conclude "the coup failed, so it changed nothing." The correct analysis: even a failed coup can permanently shift a system's equilibrium if the response to it removes moderates rather than restoring balance. Apply this framework to the incident by asking three questions: (1) Who held relative power before? (Party cabinets, civilian ministers, moderate generals.) (2) What resource did the crisis destroy or redistribute? (The remaining pool of officials willing to check military demands.) (3) Who consolidated power afterward, and by what mechanism? (The Tōseiha, through purges and the credible threat of further violence.) This same three-question test applies broadly to any attempted uprising, impeachment, or purge in history—the aftermath, not the event itself, is where power actually relocates.
