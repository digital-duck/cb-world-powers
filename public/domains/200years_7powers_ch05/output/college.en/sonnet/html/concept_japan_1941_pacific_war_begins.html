Japan 1941 Pacific War Begins

By mid-1941, Japan's military leadership faced a strategic bind of its own making. The 1937 invasion of China had bogged down into a costly, unwinnable occupation, and Japan depended on the United States for roughly 80 percent of its oil. When Japan occupied southern French Indochina in July 1941, positioning itself to strike further south, the United States responded by freezing Japanese assets and imposing a total oil embargo, joined by Britain and the Dutch government-in-exile. Japan had perhaps eighteen months of fuel reserves. Its leaders concluded they faced a choice between withdrawing from China — an unacceptable loss of face and strategic position — or seizing the oil, rubber, and other resources of Southeast Asia by force, which meant war with the United States, Britain, and the Netherlands simultaneously.

The plan that emerged, primarily under Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto and General Hideki Tojo's government, was not a single attack but a coordinated campaign across thousands of miles. On December 7–8, 1941 (local dates), Japan launched near-simultaneous strikes: a carrier-based air raid on the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii; landings in British Malaya; an assault on Hong Kong; the invasion of the Philippines (a US territory); and moves against the Dutch East Indies and other Pacific islands. The logic was to cripple American naval power in one blow while securing resource-rich territories before the US or Britain could mount an effective defense — a strategy of speed and simultaneity rather than a single decisive battle.

This event illustrates a recurring pattern in strategic decision-making: an actor facing a closing window of opportunity and a resource constraint may choose a high-risk, wide-scope action over a slow decline. Apply this lens by asking, for any crisis you study, three questions: What resource or time constraint is driving the decision? What was the rejected alternative, and why was it judged worse? And did the plan's scope (attacking multiple targets at once) match the actor's actual military and logistical capacity to sustain it? Japan's planners answered the first two questions clearly but badly misjudged the third — the campaign achieved rapid initial success but committed Japan to a war of attrition against a far larger industrial power, a mismatch that would define the war's second half.
