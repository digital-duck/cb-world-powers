Japan 1932 May 15 Incident

On May 15, 1932, eleven young naval officers, joined by army cadets and civilian ultranationalists, launched a coordinated attack on symbols of parliamentary government in Tokyo. They stormed the official residence of Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi and shot him dead; other cells threw grenades at the Bank of Japan, party headquarters, and a police station. The plotters hoped their violence would trigger a military-led "Shōwa Restoration" that would sweep away party politics and Western-style capitalism in favor of direct imperial rule guided by the armed forces. In this immediate goal they failed — no coup followed, and Japan did not become a military dictatorship overnight. But the incident permanently altered the trajectory of Japanese politics.

The event's significance lies less in what the assassins intended than in how the state responded. Rather than face a court-martial that could expose the depth of ultranationalist sentiment in the officer corps, or a civilian trial that might indict the military's political role, the government tried the assassins in a court-martial that became a public platform for their grievances. Public opinion, primed by economic depression, rural poverty, and resentment of party corruption, largely sympathized with the killers; petitions for leniency arrived by the thousands, some signed in blood. The lenient sentences that followed signaled that violence against civilian leaders carried little real cost.

The most consequential outcome was structural. After Inukai's death, no party leader was again asked to form a cabinet before 1945; instead, prime ministers were drawn from the military, the bureaucracy, or compromise figures acceptable to the armed forces. This closed the era of "Taishō democracy" party cabinets that had governed Japan since 1924.

Read as a case study in institutional breakdown, the incident illustrates a recurring pattern: when an armed faction assassinates a civilian leader without triggering firm legal or political consequences, the precedent emboldens further defiance. This same logic — junior officers acting on their own initiative, met with tacit tolerance from superiors — had already appeared in the January 28 Incident earlier that year, when the Japanese military escalated hostilities in Shanghai partly independent of civilian oversight, and it would recur with even greater force in the February 26 Incident of 1936.
