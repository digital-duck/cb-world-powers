Russia 1985 Gorbachevs Glasnost Perestroika

When Mikhail Gorbachev assumed power in 1985, the Soviet Union was suffering from acute economic stagnation and political rigidity—a failure of central planning that could not keep pace with global technological change. Recognizing that simply tightening control would lead to inevitable collapse (and remembering the military failures of the Afghanistan War), he introduced two radical, interconnected policies designed to save socialism: Perestroika (restructuring) and Glasnost (openness). Perestroika aimed at reforming the dysfunctional state-owned economy by introducing market mechanisms and decentralizing production. Glasnost sought political transparency, allowing citizens and intellectuals to criticize government failures and discuss historical grievances through a newly permitted media landscape. The fundamental premise was that the system could be saved from within if it faced genuine self-diagnosis.

A concrete example of these reforms’ mechanism in action is the Chernobyl disaster of 1986. Historically, Soviet authorities had covered up details regarding the reactor's safety issues and subsequent meltdown for years. Under Glasnost, however, state media could no longer suppress information; international press coverage and newly empowered dissident groups forced official acknowledgement. This transparency was revolutionary, exposing not just a technical failure, but decades of systemic dishonesty that undermined public trust in the Communist Party structure itself—a critical blow to legitimacy.

The enduring problem-solving application lies in understanding the unintended consequences of selective reform. The Soviet system was an interlocking machine where political authority (Party control) and economic life (central planning) were mutually reinforcing. By decoupling the two, Gorbachev initiated a cascade failure. Perestroika introduced market incentives but lacked the foundational legal and institutional structures to manage them, leading instead to supply bottlenecks and hyperinflation in key sectors like agriculture. Meanwhile, Glasnost successfully mobilized suppressed nationalist sentiments—most visibly in Baltic states—by validating local grievances against Moscow’s historical overreach. The reforms were thus not self-correcting mechanisms; they functioned as decompressors that released decades of accumulated political pressure, demonstrating how attempting to solve an economic problem (stagnation) by increasing transparency and decentralization ultimately destabilized the entire central power structure.
