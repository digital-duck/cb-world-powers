Russia 1979 Soviet Afghan War

Definition. In December 1979, the Soviet Union deployed roughly 30,000 troops into Afghanistan, installed a new leader, Babrak Karmal, and moved to prop up a fragile communist government against a growing insurgency. What Soviet planners expected to be a short stabilizing intervention became a nine-year occupation, drawing in over 100,000 troops at its peak and ending in a 1989 withdrawal that left the Afghan communist government to collapse on its own three years later.

Worked example. The war's logic can be traced through cause and consequence. The Afghan communist party had seized power in 1978 but was bitterly split into rival factions, and its land and social reforms provoked armed resistance across the countryside. When the government's own instability threatened to bring down the regime entirely, Moscow intervened — not to expand influence, but to prevent the loss of an existing client state, the same defensive logic behind [[russia_1968_prague_spring_invasion]] in Czechoslovakia eleven years earlier. But Afghanistan's mountainous terrain and armed, US- and Pakistan-backed mujahideen resistance made this a far costlier fight than Prague. Soviet forces controlled cities and highways but never pacified the countryside, and casualties, economic strain, and international isolation (including a US-led boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics) accumulated for a decade.

Problem-solving application. Historians and policy analysts use this case as a template for evaluating any great-power military intervention: does the occupying force control territory, or merely contest it? Ask three diagnostic questions the Soviet leadership answered too late. First, does the client government have independent legitimacy and administrative capacity, or does it depend entirely on foreign troops to survive? Second, does the terrain and population favor insurgency (dispersed, mountainous, tribal) or centralized control? Third, can the intervening power sustain the war's economic and political cost longer than domestic opposition can build? Applying this framework retroactively, the Soviet Afghan war fails all three tests — which is precisely why it is invoked as a comparative case when assessing later foreign interventions in Afghanistan and elsewhere, and why its financial and morale costs are counted among the pressures that accelerated the Soviet Union's collapse in 1991.
