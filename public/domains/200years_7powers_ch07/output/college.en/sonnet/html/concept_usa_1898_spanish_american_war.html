Usa 1898 Spanish American War

The Spanish-American War (1898) was a brief but decisive conflict between the United States and Spain, fought primarily in Cuba and the Philippines. It ended with a lopsided US victory: Spain ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States, and Cuba was granted nominal independence under continued US military and economic supervision. The war marked the emergence of the United States as an imperial power operating beyond its continental borders — a direct consequence of the "closing of the frontier" a decade earlier, when expansionist energy that had previously been absorbed by westward settlement now sought outlets overseas.

The immediate triggers were the sinking of the USS Maine in Havana harbor (blamed, without conclusive evidence, on Spain) and American public sympathy for Cuban rebels fighting Spanish colonial rule, amplified by sensationalist "yellow journalism." Congress declared war in April 1898. The fighting itself was short: Commodore Dewey destroyed the Spanish fleet at Manila Bay within hours, and US forces overwhelmed Spanish garrisons in Cuba by summer. The Treaty of Paris (December 1898) formalized the territorial transfers.

Applying this case requires distinguishing formal outcomes from underlying mechanisms. Ask: what specific institutional and economic returns did each territory generate? Puerto Rico and Guam became naval and logistical strongholds — the same acquisition, considered as a foothold, explains why the US held them for over a century rather than seeking early independence for them, unlike Cuba. The Philippines is the sharper case: annexation triggered the Philippine-American War (1899–1902), a costly guerrilla conflict that exposed the contradiction between America's own anti-colonial founding narrative and its acquisition of an overseas empire — a tension debated in Congress at the time (the Anti-Imperialist League vs. expansionists) and still central to how historians frame 1898.

A useful exercise: compare the "supervised independence" granted to Cuba (via the Platt Amendment, which reserved US rights to intervene) against the outright annexation of the Philippines and Puerto Rico. Why the different treatment? Consider variables like domestic Cuban political organization, US economic interests (sugar, tobacco), and geographic proximity to the US mainland versus the Pacific. This comparative framing — same war, three distinct colonial outcomes — is the analytical skill this section is meant to build: reading imperial policy not as a single monolithic decision, but as a set of context-dependent bargains.
