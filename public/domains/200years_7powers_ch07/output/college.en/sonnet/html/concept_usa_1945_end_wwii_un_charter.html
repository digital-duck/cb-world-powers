By 1945, the machinery built under the G.I. Bill era of mobilization reached its endpoint. Germany's surrender on May 8 (V-E Day) ended the war in Europe after the Allied advances of 1944–45 collapsed Nazi resistance. In the Pacific, the US had been island-hopping toward Japan for years, but Japanese military and political leadership showed no sign of accepting unconditional surrender. Facing projections of massive casualties from a planned invasion of the Japanese home islands, President Truman authorized the use of a new weapon: the atomic bomb, developed in secret by the Manhattan Project. The US dropped bombs on Hiroshima (August 6) and Nagasaki (August 9); Japan surrendered on September 2, 1945 (V-J Day), ending World War II.

The scale of destruction and the war's global reach convinced political leaders that the League of Nations model — which had failed to prevent this second world war — needed replacing. In June 1945, delegates from 50 nations, including the US, signed the United Nations Charter in San Francisco. The US Senate ratified it that summer, making the US a founding member and, notably, a permanent member of the UN Security Council alongside the UK, France, the Soviet Union, and China. This structure gave the five permanent members veto power over Security Council resolutions — a deliberate design choice meant to secure buy-in from the war's major victors, even at the cost of making the Council prone to gridlock whenever their interests diverged.

For a problem-solving application, consider evaluating institutional design rather than events themselves: why might veto power for great powers have been judged necessary despite its costs? Analyze it as a trade-off. Without the veto, a major power outvoted on a matter of vital interest might simply refuse to join or later withdraw, as had crippled the League of Nations when the US never joined and other powers exited over disputes. The veto traded some effectiveness (frequent gridlock) for durability (major powers staying inside the institution). This kind of cost-benefit reasoning — asking what problem a rule was designed to solve, and what it sacrificed to solve it — is a transferable skill for analyzing any institutional design, from constitutions to corporate governance structures.
