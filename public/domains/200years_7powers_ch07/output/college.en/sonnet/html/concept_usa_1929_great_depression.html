Usa 1929 Great Depression

The Great Depression (1929–1939) was the deepest and longest economic contraction in modern U.S. history. It began with the stock market crash of October 1929, when overvalued shares — many purchased "on margin" with borrowed money — collapsed in value, wiping out investor wealth almost overnight. But the crash was a trigger, not the sole cause: it exposed deeper structural weaknesses, including an unregulated banking system, overproduction in agriculture and industry, and dangerously unequal income distribution that had left most households without savings to absorb a shock.

The crash's effects cascaded through the economy. Panicked depositors rushed to withdraw savings, but banks held only a fraction of deposits in reserve; over 9,000 banks failed between 1930 and 1933, destroying the savings of millions and shrinking the money supply. As credit dried up, businesses cut production and laid off workers, and unemployment climbed to roughly 25% by 1933 — one in four workers idle. Falling demand and falling prices reinforced each other in a deflationary spiral: as prices dropped, businesses cut wages and jobs further, which reduced spending, which pushed prices down again.

Agriculture suffered a compounding, environmental crisis. Decades of over-plowing the Great Plains had stripped away native grasses that held soil in place; a severe drought beginning in 1930 turned exposed topsoil into massive dust storms, creating the "Dust Bowl." Tens of thousands of farm families in states like Oklahoma, Texas, and Kansas lost their land and migrated west, most famously to California, in search of work — a mass displacement later documented in Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath and Dorothea Lange's photography.

To analyze why the downturn was so severe, economists point to the interaction of a demand shock (collapsed investment and consumer spending) with a passive monetary policy: the Federal Reserve allowed the money supply to contract by about a third, rather than acting as lender of last resort to failing banks. This combination — a preventable policy failure layered on top of a market crash — became the central lesson later applied to prevent recessions like the 2008 financial crisis from becoming depressions, and it directly set up the political demand for the interventionist New Deal policies that followed.
