France 1981 Mitterrand Elected

Definition. In May 1981, François Mitterrand defeated incumbent Valéry Giscard d'Estaing to become the Fifth Republic's first Socialist president, ending twenty-three years of conservative dominance since Charles de Gaulle founded the system in 1958. Mitterrand's initial program, sometimes called the "110 Propositions," nationalized major banks and industrial groups, raised the minimum wage, shortened the workweek, and expanded social spending — a direct attempt to convert the political and cultural energy released by the May 1968 protests into structural economic change. But by 1983, facing capital flight, a weakening franc, and rising inflation, Mitterrand executed the "tournant de la rigueur" (austerity turn), abandoning expansionary Keynesianism for wage restraint, budget discipline, and closer integration with European monetary policy.

Worked example. Consider the policy sequence as a constrained optimization problem the government revises over time. In 1981, the objective function weighted employment and redistribution heavily: government spending rose roughly 27% in real terms over two years, and over a thousand companies and nearly forty banks were nationalized. The constraint that eventually bound was the balance of payments: France's inflation ran several points above West Germany's while both currencies were pegged within the European Monetary System's exchange-rate mechanism. A persistently higher French inflation rate relative to Germany meant the franc kept losing competitiveness against the deutschmark, forcing three devaluations between 1981 and 1983. Each devaluation eroded confidence and accelerated capital outflows, effectively raising the shadow price of expansionary policy until it exceeded French willingness to exit the EMS.

Problem-solving application. This episode illustrates a recurring policy trade-off: a government operating inside a fixed or semi-fixed exchange-rate regime loses an independent monetary lever, so a fiscal expansion not matched by its trading partners exports pressure onto the currency rather than purely onto output. When analyzing any policy shift like Mitterrand's 1983 reversal, ask three diagnostic questions: (1) What external constraint (exchange rate, capital mobility, treaty obligation) limited the original policy's sustainability? (2) What indicator (currency reserves, inflation differential, bond yields) signaled the constraint was binding? (3) What alternative — devaluation, exit from the regime, or policy reversal — was chosen, and why? Applying this framework to other cases (Britain's 1976 IMF crisis, or Greece post-2010) reveals a common structural logic: domestic redistributive ambitions frequently collide with the discipline imposed by international monetary commitments, and resolving that collision usually forces a choice between exiting the commitment or curbing the ambition.
