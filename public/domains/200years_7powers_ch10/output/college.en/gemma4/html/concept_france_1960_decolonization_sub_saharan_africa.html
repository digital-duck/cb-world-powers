The rapid decolonization wave across French West and Central Africa in the late 1950s and early 1960s represents a critical turning point in modern global history. Following decades of indirect rule, where local elites managed colonial administration under Parisian guidance, nearly two dozen colonies gained formal independence. This process was not a simple transfer of power; rather, it involved the sudden vacuum of authority following French withdrawal, leading to profound political instability and creating new forms of institutional dependency. While sovereignty passed to local leaders—such as those who formed independent nations like Mali (1960) or Upper Volta (now Burkina Faso)—the deep economic structures, military ties, and administrative frameworks established during the colonial period remained intact.

Consider the case of Dahomey (modern Benin). Upon independence in 1960, the new government struggled with internal divisions exacerbated by French administrative practices that had often grouped disparate ethnic communities together for ease of rule. The rapid withdrawal left a governance vacuum, leading to immediate political fragmentation and instability—a common pattern across Francophone Africa. These early crises demonstrated that while the flag changed hands, the fundamental challenges lay in nation-building itself: how to reconcile deep regional identities with the forced boundaries of the colonial state.

The critical consequence is not merely independence, but the establishment of Françafrique—the enduring network of political, economic, and military influence France maintained over its former colonies. Rather than withdrawing entirely, Paris utilized treaties, financial aid, and preferential trade agreements to ensure continued strategic access. For instance, French control was often maintained through bilateral agreements regarding infrastructure (like ports or railways) or by supporting pro-French political elites who could guarantee predictable economic relationships for major European corporations. The problem here is not one of failed independence, but of structural dependency: the newly sovereign state becomes economically and militarily tethered to its former metropole. Analyzing this dynamic requires identifying how continued French institutional involvement—be it through military cooperation or financial aid tied to specific policy choices—mitigated genuine self-determination, effectively transforming political sovereignty into functional dependence for decades to come.
