Usa 1991 Gulf War Soviet Collapse

Definition. Two events in 1991 reset the structure of world power. First, Iraq's August 1990 invasion of Kuwait — a smaller oil-producing neighbor — triggered a US-led, UN-authorized coalition of roughly 35 nations. In January–February 1991, Operation Desert Storm expelled Iraqi forces in a six-week campaign combining air supremacy, precision-guided munitions, and overwhelming coalition ground forces. Second, and independently, the Soviet Union — weakened by economic stagnation, the failed 1991 August coup against Gorbachev, and rising nationalism in its constituent republics — formally dissolved in December 1991, splitting into fifteen independent states, with Russia inheriting its UN Security Council seat and nuclear arsenal. Though causally unrelated, the two events landed in the same calendar year and jointly closed the Cold War era: one superpower vanished from the board while the other demonstrated, in Kuwait, an unmatched capacity to organize and project military power globally.

Worked example. Consider how these events reshaped incentives for other states. Before 1991, a country deciding whether to align with Washington or Moscow was making a bet between two competing patrons offering economic aid, arms, and security guarantees. After 1991, that calculation changed: only one patron remained capable of underwriting security commitments on a global scale, and the Gulf War had just supplied a public demonstration of what that capability looked like in practice. A regional power weighing whether to invade a neighbor, as Iraq had, now had to price in the credible risk of a broad international coalition assembling in response — a risk that had been diluted for decades by superpower rivalry and vetoes.

Problem-solving application. When analyzing why the 1990s became known as a "unipolar moment," identify the two necessary conditions this section illustrates: (1) removal of a peer competitor (Soviet collapse) and (2) demonstrated, not merely claimed, military-diplomatic capacity (the Gulf War coalition). Apply this two-part test to later cases — for instance, evaluating claims of "multipolarity" in later decades requires checking whether a rising power satisfies both conditions, or only one (e.g., growing economic weight without a comparable coalition-building or force-projection track record).
