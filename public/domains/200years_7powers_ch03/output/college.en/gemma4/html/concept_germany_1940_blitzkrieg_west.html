Germany 1940 Blitzkrieg West

The term Blitzkrieg ("lightning war") describes the German military doctrine and tactical implementation used during the invasions of Denmark, Norway (Operation Weserübung), Belgium, and France in May–June 1940. Far from being merely a rush of men, it was a highly coordinated system emphasizing speed, maneuverability, and shock action across multiple domains simultaneously. Operationally, Blitzkrieg rejected the rigid, frontal attrition warfare that characterized previous eras. Instead, it utilized mechanized forces—specifically armored divisions (Panzer) and motorized infantry—to bypass heavily defended positions and strike deep into enemy rear areas. This bypassed approach was coupled with immediate air support from the Luftwaffe, which provided continuous targeting data against opposing communications, command centers, and supply lines. The goal was not to conquer every kilometer, but to rapidly dismantle an opponent's ability to coordinate a unified defense by paralyzing its leadership infrastructure.

Worked Example: The Fall of France. When Germany invaded France after passing through neutral Belgium, the mechanism came into sharp focus. German forces did not engage in prolonged sieges at the French border. Instead, they executed a sweeping thrust (the Sickle Cut) through the Ardennes forest and toward the Channel coast. This maneuver bypassed the heavily fortified Maginot Line complex further east. By striking through unexpected routes—routes that forced Allied armies into unplanned defensive maneuvers—Germany severed major supply lines. The combined effect of rapid armored penetration, supported by overwhelming air superiority (which targeted railway nodes and fuel depots), caused the Allied forces to break down structurally before they could fight a decisive battle on German terms.

Problem-Solving Application: Analyzing Operational Success. To understand how Blitzkrieg succeeded against militaries designed for static defense, one must analyze its critical components as an integrated protocol rather than separate actions. The success was not due solely to the tanks, but to the synchronization of three elements: 1) Speed and Mobility: Using motorized transport to dictate the pace of engagement; 2) Air Supremacy: Utilizing the Luftwaffe to suppress enemy counter-attacks and dismantle infrastructure (e.g., bombing rail junctions); and 3) Decentralized Command: Empowering divisional commanders (especially Panzer units) with tactical autonomy, allowing them to exploit unexpected gaps in defenses immediately—a flexibility severely lacking in many established Allied commands facing a rapid operational tempo. The critical turning point was the speed of communication failure; by disrupting railways and command posts, Germany prevented coordinated reactions, transforming large armies into isolated pockets of resistance that were easy targets for subsequent mechanized sweeps.
