Germany 1934 Night Long Knives

By mid-1934, Hitler's grip on Germany's political institutions was secure, but two rival power centers still threatened his authority. The Sturmabteilung (SA), the Nazi Party's paramilitary "brownshirts," numbered nearly three million men under Ernst Röhm, who wanted the SA merged with — and placed above — the traditional German army (the Reichswehr). Meanwhile, conservative elites, including Vice-Chancellor Franz von Papen and figures around aging President Paul von Hindenburg, watched Nazi radicalism with growing unease. Hitler needed the army's loyalty to secure the succession after Hindenburg's death, and the army's generals despised Röhm's ambitions. This created an opening: eliminate the SA leadership and buy the army's allegiance in one stroke.

On June 30–July 2, 1934, SS and Gestapo units arrested and executed dozens of people without trial — Röhm and the SA's top leadership, but also unrelated political enemies, including former Chancellor Kurt von Schleicher and conservative critic Gustav Ritter von Kahr. Estimates of the dead range from about 85 to over 200. The killings were carried out entirely outside any judicial process: no charges, no trials, no appeals — decisions made and executed by the party's own security apparatus.

The purge's significance lies less in the body count than in what it revealed about the Nazi state's relationship to law. Only weeks later, the Reichstag retroactively legalized the killings as "emergency defensive measures," folding extrajudicial violence into the same legal-seeming framework established by the Reichstag Fire Decree and Enabling Act. This is the throughline connecting the two events: the Enabling Act had already dissolved the separation between executive will and law, and the Night of the Long Knives demonstrated that this fused power now extended to matters of life and death, executed by party organs rather than courts.

The political payoff was immediate. The army, freed from SA competition, swore personal oaths of loyalty to Hitler after Hindenburg's death that August, and conservative elites who had hoped to restrain Hitler learned that opposition — even from within his own movement — could be answered with assassination rather than negotiation. In problem-solving terms: when analyzing any authoritarian consolidation, look for the moment when a leader eliminates the faction that helped bring him to power, converts a private militia's violence into an instrument of state policy, and secures the military's loyalty as the price of that violence — the sequence that "protective legality" (1933) followed by "protective violence" (1934) establishes as a durable pattern for eliminating rivals without formal legal risk.
