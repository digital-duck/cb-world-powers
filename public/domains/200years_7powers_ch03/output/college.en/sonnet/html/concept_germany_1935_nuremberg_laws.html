Germany 1935 Nuremberg Laws

Definition. The Nuremberg Laws, enacted by the Nazi government on September 15, 1935, consisted of two statutes: the Reich Citizenship Law and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honor. The first stripped Jews of German citizenship, reclassifying them as mere "subjects" of the state with no political rights. The second criminalized marriage and sexual relations between Jews and "persons of German or related blood," and forbade Jews from employing German female domestic servants under 45. A supplementary decree later defined who counted as "Jewish" using ancestry — not religious practice — establishing categories such as "full Jew," "half Jew" (Mischling of the first degree), and "quarter Jew," based on the religious affiliation of one's grandparents.

Worked example. Consider the bureaucratic logic these laws imposed: a person's legal status no longer depended on citizenship documents, religious self-identification, or personal conduct, but on a genealogical audit reaching back two generations. Someone who had never practiced Judaism, whose parents had converted to Christianity decades earlier, could still be classified as Jewish if enough grandparents were registered as Jewish. This is the defining feature of racial (as opposed to religious or cultural) persecution: identity is assigned externally, retroactively, and irrevocably by the state, using a rule the individual cannot alter through belief or behavior.

Problem-solving application: recognizing legal-institutional escalation. The historical significance of the Nuremberg Laws lies in what they represent structurally, not just factually. They mark the shift from extralegal violence (the SA's street terror, the purge in the [[germany_1934_night_long_knives]]) to codified legal exclusion — antisemitism written into statute, enforced by courts, police, and civil registries rather than mobs. This distinction matters analytically: once discrimination is embedded in law, it becomes self-enforcing and bureaucratically scalable — every clerk, employer, and marriage registrar becomes an instrument of the policy without further orders. When analyzing any authoritarian regime's trajectory, ask whether persecution is ad hoc (reversible, contested, deniable) or legally formalized (systemic, durable, administratively automated). The Nuremberg Laws answer that question for Nazi Germany: 1935 is the year state antisemitism became infrastructure, a precondition — though not yet a mechanism — for the mass deportations and killings that followed.
