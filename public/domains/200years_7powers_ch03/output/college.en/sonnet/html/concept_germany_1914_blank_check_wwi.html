When Archduke Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, by a Bosnian Serb nationalist, Austria-Hungary wanted to punish Serbia but feared Russia would intervene on Serbia's behalf. On July 5, 1914, Kaiser Wilhelm II's government gave Austria-Hungary the "blank check" — an unconditional pledge of German military support no matter how harshly Vienna dealt with Serbia. This was a decisive escalation: it removed Austria-Hungary's restraint, turning a regional crisis into a continental one.

Emboldened, Austria-Hungary issued Serbia an ultimatum with terms designed to be rejected. When Serbia's response fell short, Austria-Hungary declared war on July 28. Russia mobilized to defend its fellow Slavic state, triggering the alliance system: Germany declared war on Russia, then on Russia's ally France. To defeat France quickly before Russia could fully mobilize, Germany executed the Schlieffen Plan — a pre-war strategy calling for a rapid sweep through neutral Belgium to outflank French defenses and encircle Paris. Belgium's neutrality had been guaranteed by an 1839 treaty that Britain had co-signed; the German invasion of Belgium on August 3–4 gave Britain both a legal pretext and a strategic reason (an unchecked Germany dominating the Channel coast threatened British security) to enter the war against Germany the next day.

The blank check illustrates a recurring problem in international crisis management: when a stronger power offers a weaker ally unconditional backing, it removes the ally's incentive for restraint, shifting control of the crisis's escalation to whichever side is least cautious. Apply this reasoning to the July Crisis as a whole. Trace the sequence of moves — assassination, blank check, ultimatum, mobilization, invasion — and identify at which step a different choice by Germany or Austria-Hungary might have contained the conflict to the Balkans rather than triggering a general European war. Consider also why the Schlieffen Plan's fixed timetable, designed for a two-front war against France and Russia, made diplomatic backpedaling nearly impossible once mobilization began: military planning had effectively outpaced political control, a pattern worth recognizing in any system where rigid pre-committed strategies interact with fast-moving crises.
