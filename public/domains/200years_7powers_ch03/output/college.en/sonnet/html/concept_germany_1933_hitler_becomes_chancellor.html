Germany 1933 Hitler Becomes Chancellor

By January 1933, Germany's parliamentary system had effectively stalled. The Great Depression had pushed unemployment past six million, and no coalition in the Reichstag could command a stable majority. The Nazi Party, though it had lost seats in the November 1932 election, remained the largest bloc. President Paul von Hindenburg's inner circle, led by former chancellor Franz von Papen, believed they could use Hitler as a figurehead: appoint him chancellor, surround his cabinet with conservative ministers, and control him from within. On January 30, 1933, Hindenburg swore Hitler in as chancellor of a coalition cabinet in which Nazis held only three of eleven posts.

This calculation collapsed almost immediately. Hitler used the chancellorship's control over the police and the machinery of government to consolidate power far faster than his partners expected. The Reichstag fire on February 27, 1933, provided the pretext for the Reichstag Fire Decree, suspending civil liberties and enabling mass arrests of Communist and Socialist opponents. Weeks later, the Enabling Act let Hitler's cabinet enact laws without Reichstag approval, dismantling the constitutional checks that might have contained him. Within months, other parties were banned, labor unions dissolved, and the states' governments subordinated to Berlin — a process historians call Gleichschaltung, or forced coordination.

The foreign-policy consequences followed the same trajectory. In October 1933, Germany withdrew from the League of Nations and the ongoing disarmament conference, signaling its rejection of the postwar settlement. Rearmament, still covert at first, accelerated openly after 1935, violating the Treaty of Versailles with little effective response from Britain or France.

This sequence — economic crisis, elite miscalculation, legal capture of the state, then external revisionism — offers a case study for evaluating political transitions generally: ask not only whether a leader gained power through nominally legal means, but whether the institutions meant to check that power (courts, legislatures, a free press) remained functionally independent afterward. Applying that framework to 1933 clarifies why Hindenburg's advisers' assumption of control proved catastrophically wrong: legal appointment and institutional capture are separable events, and the second can follow the first within weeks once emergency powers exist to be invoked.
