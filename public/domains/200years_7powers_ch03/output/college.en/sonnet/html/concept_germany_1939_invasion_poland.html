Germany 1939 Invasion Poland

By 1939, Nazi Germany's anti-Jewish violence had escalated from legal discrimination to open terror — the November 1938 Kristallnacht pogroms had already signaled that Hitler's regime would pursue its ideological goals through force, not diplomacy. That same logic now extended to foreign policy. Having annexed Austria and dismembered Czechoslovakia without triggering war, Hitler calculated that Poland could be seized the same way — but Britain and France had by 1939 pledged to defend Polish sovereignty, raising the stakes of any invasion to continental war.

The decisive diplomatic maneuver came on August 23, 1939, when Germany and the Soviet Union signed the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, a non-aggression treaty negotiated by foreign ministers Vyacheslav Molotov and Joachim von Ribbentrop. Publicly, it bound the two ideological enemies — fascist Germany and communist USSR — to non-aggression. Secretly, it included a protocol dividing Poland and the Baltic states into German and Soviet "spheres of influence." This eliminated the two-front war Germany had feared since World War I: with the USSR neutralized, Hitler could attack Poland without risking Soviet intervention.

On September 1, 1939, Germany invaded Poland, deploying a coordinated assault of fast-moving armored columns and air power — later termed Blitzkrieg — that overwhelmed Polish defenses within weeks. Britain and France, honoring their guarantee to Poland, declared war on Germany on September 3, formally starting World War II in Europe. Sixteen days after the German invasion began, Soviet forces entered eastern Poland under the pact's secret terms, completing the country's partition.

To analyze this turning point, distinguish the pact's stated terms from its function: it was not a friendship agreement but a temporary mutual-convenience arrangement that let each dictator avoid a two-front conflict while pursuing separate expansion — Germany westward, the USSR into Eastern Europe. When examining any historical alliance, ask three questions: what does each party publicly commit to, what do they privately gain, and what does the agreement enable that neither could achieve alone? Applied here, the pact reveals how tactical cooperation between ideological rivals can still produce catastrophic consequences — the invasion of Poland that followed eleven days later.
