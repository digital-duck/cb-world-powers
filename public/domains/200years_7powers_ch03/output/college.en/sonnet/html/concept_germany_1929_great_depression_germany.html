The Great Depression in Germany (1929–1932) transformed a fragile recovery into a systemic collapse of the Weimar Republic's economic and political order. Germany's mid-1920s stabilization had depended on short-term American loans, arranged under the Dawes Plan, that financed reparations payments, industrial investment, and government spending. When the Wall Street stock market crashed in October 1929, US banks called in these loans to cover their own losses. Germany's economy, structurally dependent on this foreign capital, had no cushion to absorb the shock.

The consequences were severe and rapid. Industrial production fell by roughly 40 percent between 1929 and 1932. Banks failed, most dramatically the collapse of the Darmstädter und Nationalbank in 1931, which triggered a broader banking panic. Unemployment climbed past six million by 1932 — nearly a third of the workforce — and government relief systems, already strained, could not keep pace. Chancellor Heinrich Brüning responded with austerity measures — cutting wages, benefits, and public spending — reasoning that fiscal discipline would restore international confidence. Instead, the deflationary spiral deepened the crisis, since falling wages and government cutbacks further reduced demand, closing more businesses and adding more unemployed workers.

The political effects proved decisive. Voters facing unemployment, bank failures, and collapsing savings increasingly abandoned the moderate parties that had governed the Republic. The Nazi Party's Reichstag seat share jumped from 12 seats in 1928 to 107 in 1930 and 230 in July 1932, making it the largest party in parliament. The Communist Party (KPD) grew in parallel, drawing support from the same pool of economically desperate voters. This polarization hollowed out the political center: coalition governments became unworkable, and Brüning increasingly governed by presidential emergency decree under Article 48, bypassing the Reichstag entirely — a precedent that normalized rule without parliamentary majority.

Applying this to broader analysis: the German case illustrates how external economic shocks translate into domestic political radicalization when a system lacks buffers — here, a democracy less than a decade old, an economy dependent on foreign credit, and a reparations burden limiting fiscal flexibility. When comparing interwar crises, look for this same mechanism: economic collapse removing the material basis for political moderation, pushing voters toward parties promising radical, total solutions rather than incremental reform.
