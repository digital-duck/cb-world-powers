Germany 1936 Remilitarization Rhineland

The Rhineland was a strip of German territory along the French border that the Treaty of Versailles (1919) and the Locarno Pact (1925) had made demilitarized: Germany retained sovereignty over the land but was forbidden from stationing troops or building fortifications there. The buffer existed to give France early warning and a defensive cushion against any future German attack. On March 7, 1936, Adolf Hitler ordered roughly 22,000 German troops into the Rhineland, openly violating both treaties. The gamble was calculated but risky — German military leaders themselves warned that France's army alone could have crushed the move.

Worked example. Consider the decision from the perspective of French and British policymakers weighing whether to respond militarily. France had a large standing army and, on paper, an overwhelming advantage; German forces would likely have withdrawn under real pressure. Yet France would not act without British support, and Britain viewed the Rhineland as "Germany's own back garden" — territory many British officials thought Germany had a right to defend, even if the method was illegal. This asymmetry between capability and willingness is the crux of the case: possessing the power to enforce a rule is not the same as having the political will to use it. Both governments also feared repeating the destruction of World War I over a step that, however illegal, did not yet threaten their own borders.

Problem-solving application. This episode is a foundational case study in analyzing deterrence failure, a recurring problem across international relations, negotiation, and security studies. To evaluate any deterrence situation, you must separate three distinct variables: the deterring power's capability (can it act?), its credibility (will observers believe it would act?), and the cost calculus facing the challenger (does the expected gain outweigh the expected punishment?). Applying this framework here: French capability was high, but credibility was near zero because domestic politics, alliance dependence on Britain, and war-weariness signaled that France would tolerate a fait accompli. Hitler's intelligence correctly read this gap. The lesson generalizes beyond 1936: whenever you assess whether a rule, sanction, or treaty will actually be enforced, ask not "can the enforcer punish this violation?" but "will they, given the costs they perceive?" — a distinction that explains why later German expansions (Austria, the Sudetenland) proceeded along the same low-credibility opening the Rhineland had exposed.
