Germany 1941 Operation Barbarossa

On June 22, 1941, Germany launched Operation Barbarossa, invading the Soviet Union along a front roughly 2,900 kilometers long with about 3.8 million Axis troops, over 3,000 tanks, and 2,700 aircraft — the largest military invasion in history. The attack broke the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact of 1939, under which the two powers had agreed not to attack each other and had jointly partitioned Poland. Hitler's aims were ideological and material at once: destroy the Soviet state as the perceived center of "Judeo-Bolshevism," seize the agricultural land of Ukraine and the oil fields of the Caucasus, and open territory for German settlement under the Generalplan Ost.

Barbarossa scaled up the blitzkrieg doctrine Germany had used against France in 1940 (see [[germany_1940_blitzkrieg_west]]) — fast-moving armored spearheads (Panzer groups) supported by close air cover from the Luftwaffe, encircling and destroying Soviet armies before they could organize a coherent defense. In the war's opening months this worked with devastating effect: German forces advanced hundreds of kilometers, captured over 3 million Soviet prisoners, and destroyed thousands of Soviet aircraft on the ground. Army Group North approached Leningrad, Army Group Center drove toward Moscow, and Army Group South pushed into Ukraine, taking Kiev in September 1941 in the largest encirclement battle in history, with roughly 650,000 Red Army soldiers captured.

But the same doctrine that had shattered France in six weeks could not deliver a comparable result across the vastness of Russia. Three factors converted a tactical triumph into a strategic failure. First, distance: Germany's supply lines, dependent on trucks and a rail gauge incompatible with Soviet track, stretched to the breaking point as the advance continued. Second, time: the invasion began five weeks later than originally planned, partly due to a diversion into the Balkans, pushing the campaign into the brutal Russian winter before German forces — unequipped for sub-zero temperatures — could take Moscow. Third, underestimation: German planners assumed the USSR would collapse within weeks, but Soviet industrial capacity, relocated eastward beyond the Urals, and Soviet manpower reserves proved far deeper than anticipated. By December 1941, the Wehrmacht stood within sight of Moscow's suburbs but was halted and pushed back by a Soviet counteroffensive — the first major German defeat of the war and the point at which Barbarossa's core objective, a swift Soviet collapse, became permanently unattainable. The Eastern Front would go on to consume the majority of German military resources for the rest of the war.
