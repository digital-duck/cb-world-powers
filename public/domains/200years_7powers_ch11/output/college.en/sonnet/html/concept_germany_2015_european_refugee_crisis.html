Germany 2015 European Refugee Crisis

In the summer of 2015, an unprecedented number of people fleeing the Syrian civil war, along with conflicts in Afghanistan and Iraq, moved across the Balkans toward Western Europe. Hungary's attempt to seal its border with fencing and closed train stations produced a humanitarian crisis at Budapest's Keleti station and along Balkan highways. On August 31, 2015, Chancellor Angela Merkel — already the dominant figure in German politics a decade after taking office (see [[germany_2005_angela_merkel_becomes_chancellor]]) — responded by suspending Dublin Regulation returns for Syrian asylum seekers and declaring "Wir schaffen das" ("We can do this"). Over the following year, Germany registered more than a million asylum applications, by far the largest reception effort of any EU state during the crisis.

Worked example. Consider why this was a genuine policy gamble rather than a symbolic gesture. Under the EU's Dublin Regulation, asylum seekers are normally required to apply in the first member state they enter — typically Greece, Italy, or Hungary given Mediterranean and Balkan routes. Merkel's decision effectively let Syrians bypass that rule and file claims directly in Germany, which removed the incentive to stop at the border and instead pulled migration flows northward. Domestically, this required Germany to scale asylum processing, housing, language instruction, and labor-market integration for over a million people in roughly eighteen months, straining municipal budgets and administrative capacity.

Problem-solving application. The consequences illustrate a recurring pattern in comparative politics: a leader's response to a sudden humanitarian shock can outlast the shock itself. The open-door stance strengthened Germany's international standing and filled labor shortages in an aging economy, but it also fueled the rise of the far-right Alternative für Deutschland (AfD), which surged from a fringe party into a significant electoral force in subsequent state and federal elections. It also strained the Christian Democratic Union's internal coalition, particularly its Bavarian sister party, the CSU, which pushed for tighter limits. Evaluating this case requires weighing humanitarian obligation against political sustainability — a trade-off analysts still cite when comparing how European states manage migration shocks, and one that reshaped German party politics well into the following decade.
