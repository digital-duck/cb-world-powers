Germany 1945 Nuremberg Trials

The Nuremberg Trials answered a question no international court had squarely faced before: could individuals be punished for crimes committed while acting as agents of a sovereign state? Under the London Charter of August 8, 1945, signed by the US, UK, France, and USSR, the International Military Tribunal (IMT) opened in Nuremberg in November 1945 to try 24 senior Nazi officials on four counts — conspiracy to wage aggressive war, crimes against peace, war crimes, and the newly defined "crimes against humanity." The choice of Nuremberg was symbolic: the city had hosted the Nazi Party's mass rallies and given its name to the 1935 racial laws stripping Jews of citizenship.

The defendants included Hermann Göring (Hitler's designated successor and Luftwaffe chief), Rudolf Hess (deputy Führer), Albert Speer (armaments minister), and Joachim von Ribbentrop (foreign minister). Hitler, Himmler, and Goebbels had already died by suicide and never faced the tribunal — a reminder that even total military defeat does not automatically deliver accountability; the process still had to be built from scratch. Over nearly a year of proceedings, prosecutors introduced captured German documents, films of concentration camps, and survivor testimony as evidence — a deliberate strategy to create an irrefutable documentary record, not merely to secure convictions. On October 1, 1946, the tribunal delivered its verdicts: twelve death sentences, three life terms, four lesser prison sentences, and three acquittals. Göring evaded the gallows only by swallowing a smuggled cyanide capsule the night before his scheduled hanging on October 16, 1946.

The trial's deeper significance was doctrinal rather than punitive. The tribunal rejected the defense that officials were merely "following orders" or that a head of state's acts on behalf of the nation were beyond personal legal reach — establishing that individual criminal liability attaches even to state-directed conduct. This became a template applied the following year at the Tokyo War Crimes Trial for Japanese wartime leaders, and it shaped later international law: the 1948 Genocide Convention, the ad hoc tribunals for Rwanda and the former Yugoslavia in the 1990s, and the permanent International Criminal Court established in 2002 all trace their conceptual lineage to the "crimes against humanity" framework first codified at Nuremberg.
