Germany 2025 Federal Election Merz Chancellorship

Definition. After the Scholz-led coalition (SPD, Greens, FDP) collapsed in late 2024 amid disputes over budget policy and the war in Ukraine, Germany held a snap federal election in early 2025. The center-right CDU/CSU bloc, campaigning on economic revival and stricter migration policy, won the largest share of seats. Friedrich Merz, leader of the CDU, was subsequently elected Chancellor by the Bundestag and formed a new coalition government. This event sits downstream of the 2022 energy crisis and recession: years of high energy costs, industrial slowdown, and public frustration with the incumbent coalition's management of these pressures eroded confidence in the governing parties and created the political opening the CDU/CSU exploited.

Worked example. Consider how a coalition government's survival depends on maintaining a working majority in the Bundestag. If a governing coalition holds 316 of 630 seats, it operates with a majority of only 1 seat. A single defection, resignation, or unresolved policy dispute — such as a budget disagreement, which is precisely what triggered the Scholz coalition's collapse — can eliminate that majority and force either a confidence vote or new elections. This illustrates why coalition governments in proportional-representation systems like Germany's are structurally more fragile during periods of economic strain: the same policy question (how much to borrow or spend to offset an energy-driven recession) that unites a coalition during stable times can split it once economic pressure exposes underlying disagreements between coalition partners over debt, spending priorities, and industrial support.

Problem-solving application. When analyzing any transition of power following an economic shock, ask three questions: (1) What structural vulnerability existed before the shock? (2) What specific policy failure or disagreement did the shock expose or intensify? (3) Who benefited politically from the resulting instability, and why? Applied here: the structural vulnerability was a multi-party coalition needing unanimous agreement on contested fiscal questions; the exposed failure was disagreement over financing responses to the energy crisis and recession; and the beneficiary was the CDU/CSU, whose platform directly addressed voter anxiety about the economy. This three-question framework generalizes to analyzing coalition collapses and snap elections in any parliamentary democracy.
