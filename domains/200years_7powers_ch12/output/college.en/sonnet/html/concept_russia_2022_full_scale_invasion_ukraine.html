Russia 2022 Full Scale Invasion Ukraine

On February 24, 2022, Russia launched a multi-front invasion of Ukraine, sending troops from Belarus toward Kyiv, from Russia toward Kharkiv, and from occupied Crimea into southern Ukraine. Unlike the covert, deniable operation Russia used in 2014, this was an overt conventional war involving hundreds of thousands of troops, air strikes on cities, and an attempted rapid decapitation of the Ukrainian government. It became the largest state-on-state war in Europe since 1945, both in troop numbers and in the scale of destruction.

The invasion is best understood as the escalation of a conflict that began with the 2014 annexation of Crimea. That earlier operation established Russia's willingness to seize Ukrainian territory and tested how the international community would respond; the sanctions imposed then were comparatively narrow and did not deter further action. By 2022, Russian President Vladimir Putin argued — inaccurately, according to most historians and international law scholars — that Ukraine was not a legitimate independent nation and that NATO's eastward expansion threatened Russian security. These justifications framed a war of territorial conquest as defensive, a pattern of escalating from limited, deniable aggression to open warfare once the earlier action went largely unpunished.

The invasion's early phase failed: Ukrainian forces, aided by Western intelligence and weapons, repelled the assault on Kyiv within weeks, forcing Russia to retreat and refocus on eastern and southern Ukraine (the Donbas and areas near Crimea). This produced a grinding war of attrition rather than the quick victory Russia had planned.

In response, the United States, European Union, and allied nations imposed sanctions far more severe than those following 2014: freezing Russian central bank assets, excluding major Russian banks from the SWIFT international payment system, banning imports of Russian oil and gas in many countries, and restricting technology exports. Analyzing this progression is a useful exercise in evaluating deterrence: ask why the 2014 sanctions failed to prevent 2022, and whether the 2022 sanctions' severity reflects that earlier lesson or the invasion's larger, harder-to-ignore scale. Consider also how the eight-year gap allowed Russia to prepare economically (building reserves, deepening trade with China) — evidence that anticipated countermeasures shape a state's calculation of whether escalation is worth the cost.
