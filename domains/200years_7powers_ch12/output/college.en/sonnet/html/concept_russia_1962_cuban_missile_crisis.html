Russia 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis

In October 1962, U-2 reconnaissance flights revealed Soviet medium- and intermediate-range ballistic missiles under construction in Cuba, roughly ninety miles from the Florida coast. Premier Nikita Khrushchev had ordered the deployment to offset the strategic imbalance created by U.S. Jupiter missiles stationed in Turkey and Italy, and to deter another invasion attempt following the failed 1961 Bay of Pigs landing. President John F. Kennedy convened an emergency advisory group, later known as ExComm, to weigh a response. The missiles, once operational, would place most major U.S. cities within a five-minute strike radius, collapsing the warning time that both superpowers' nuclear postures had assumed.

ExComm's deliberations illustrate a core problem in crisis decision-making: choosing among options that each carry unacceptable risk. A full-scale invasion of Cuba risked triggering Soviet retaliation in Berlin or a direct missile launch. Doing nothing left the missiles in place, permanently altering the strategic balance. Kennedy selected a middle path — a naval "quarantine" (avoiding the legal term "blockade," which under international law constitutes an act of war) intercepting Soviet ships bound for Cuba, paired with an ultimatum demanding missile withdrawal. This combined military signaling with a diplomatic off-ramp, buying time without forcing an immediate confrontation.

The crisis peaked on October 27, when a U-2 was shot down over Cuba and a U.S. Navy destroyer forced a Soviet submarine to surface, each moment carrying real potential to spiral into open war. Resolution came through parallel channels: publicly, Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles in exchange for a U.S. pledge not to invade Cuba; privately, Kennedy's brother Robert negotiated a secret, later-fulfilled promise to remove the Jupiter missiles from Turkey. This dual-track deal let both leaders claim a defensible outcome domestically while avoiding the appearance of capitulation.

The crisis's chief legacy was procedural rather than territorial: it exposed how fragile crisis communication was — the exchange of formal messages took hours amid rapidly evolving events — and led directly to the 1963 Moscow–Washington hotline and the Partial Test Ban Treaty. For students of foreign policy, the case remains a benchmark for analyzing how decision-makers structure choices under time pressure, incomplete information, and the shadow of catastrophic consequences.
