Russia 1985 Gorbachevs Glasnost Perestroika

By 1985, the Soviet Union was straining under the costs revealed by the war in Russia_1979_Soviet_Afghan_War: a decade-long military campaign that drained the treasury, killed roughly 15,000 Soviet soldiers, and exposed the army's limits against a lightly armed insurgency. When Mikhail Gorbachev became General Secretary that March, he inherited an economy growing at barely 2% annually, chronic consumer shortages, and a political system where the Communist Party's monopoly on truth had bred cynicism and stagnation. Gorbachev's response was two linked reform programs: glasnost ("openness"), which relaxed censorship and allowed public criticism of officials and history, and perestroika ("restructuring"), which introduced limited market mechanisms — permitting small private cooperatives, giving factory managers more autonomy, and reducing central planning quotas.

The intent was to save socialism by making it more efficient and legitimate, not to end it. Gorbachev believed that airing problems honestly and loosening bureaucratic control would re-energize the system, much as Khrushchev's de-Stalinization had briefly done a generation earlier. Instead, the reforms produced the opposite of stability. Glasnost let long-suppressed grievances surface: Soviet citizens read investigative journalism exposing famine deaths under Stalin, corruption among the elite, and the true cost of the Chernobyl disaster (1986), which Gorbachev's own government had initially concealed. Perestroika, meanwhile, disrupted the old command economy without yet building functioning markets to replace it — supply chains broke down, shortages worsened, and inflation crept upward, since price controls remained while production incentives shifted.

The practical lesson is how reform can outrun the regime's capacity to control it. Once glasnost normalized public criticism, nationalist movements in the Baltic republics (Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania) and elsewhere used the new openness to demand independence rather than mere reform — something impossible under strict censorship. Economic dissatisfaction gave these movements mass support. By 1989, Gorbachev's decision not to use force to prop up Eastern European communist governments (unlike 1956 Hungary or 1968 Czechoslovakia) allowed the Berlin Wall to fall peacefully, and by 1991 the same logic of openness and decentralization that had begun as internal repair had dissolved the Union itself. To analyze reform movements generally: distinguish reforms intended to strengthen a system from those that inadvertently remove the coercive mechanisms holding it together — Gorbachev's case shows these can be the same policy.
