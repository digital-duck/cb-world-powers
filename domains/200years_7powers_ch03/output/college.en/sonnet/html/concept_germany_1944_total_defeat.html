Germany 1944 Total Defeat

By 1944, Germany faced a war on two fronts collapsing simultaneously. On June 6, 1944 (D-Day), American, British, and Canadian forces landed in Normandy, opening the long-promised "Second Front" in the West. Meanwhile, the Soviet Red Army launched Operation Bagration that same month, destroying an entire German Army Group in Belarus and pushing westward through Poland toward Germany itself. This pincer movement — Allied forces closing from both East and West — defines the military logic of 1944–1945: Germany could no longer shift resources between fronts to hold either one, and both were advancing at once.

The consequences for the Holocaust were direct and urgent. As Soviet troops approached death and concentration camps in occupied Poland (including Auschwitz-Birkenau, liberated in January 1945), the SS attempted to destroy evidence, evacuate prisoners, and continue killing operations even as military defeat became certain. This produced the horror of the "death marches" — forced evacuations of camp prisoners westward, ahead of the advancing Soviet lines, under conditions that killed tens of thousands more. The genocide did not stop because Germany was losing the war; it accelerated in some places and only ended with physical liberation by Allied troops.

By spring 1945, Soviet forces reached Berlin while American, British, and French forces advanced from the west, meeting Soviet troops at the Elbe River in April 1945. Adolf Hitler died by suicide on April 30, 1945, as Soviet troops fought through the city. Germany's military leadership signed instruments of unconditional surrender on May 7–8, 1945, ending the war in Europe (V-E Day).

For problem-solving purposes, this period illustrates a recurring pattern in analyzing collapsing regimes: military defeat and ideological objectives can move on separate, sometimes conflicting timelines. When evaluating any historical case of state violence during a war's end phase, ask two distinct questions rather than one — "What was the military trajectory?" and "What happened to the regime's targeted victims during that same window?" — because assuming that battlefield defeat automatically halts atrocity, as the death marches demonstrate, produces an incomplete and often mistaken account.
