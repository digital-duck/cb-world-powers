Germany 1938 Anschluss Sudetenland

By 1938, Hitler's foreign policy had shifted from covert treaty violations to open territorial expansion, building directly on the precedent set by the [[germany_1936_remilitarization_rhineland|remilitarization of the Rhineland]]. That earlier crisis had demonstrated a crucial lesson to Berlin: Britain and France, though nominally committed to enforcing the post-World War I settlement, would not risk war to stop German revisionism if a plausible justification existed. In 1938, Hitler applied that lesson twice in rapid succession.

In March 1938, Germany annexed Austria in what is known as the Anschluss (German for "union" or "connection"). Nazi propaganda framed this as the natural unification of German-speaking peoples divided by the Treaty of Versailles, and Austrian Nazis inside the country had already been agitating for merger. German troops crossed the border, Austrian Chancellor Kurt Schuschnigg was forced to resign, and a plebiscite—held under conditions of open intimidation—reported over 99 percent support for union with Germany. No foreign power intervened.

Hitler next turned to the Sudetenland, a border region of Czechoslovakia with a large ethnic German population. He claimed these Germans were being persecuted and demanded the territory's transfer to Germany, threatening war if refused. To avert a general European conflict, British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain, French Premier Édouard Daladier, Hitler, and Italian dictator Benito Mussolini met in Munich in September 1938. The resulting Munich Agreement ceded the Sudetenland to Germany—without Czechoslovak participation in the negotiations. Chamberlain declared he had secured "peace for our time."

For problem-solving purposes, this sequence is best analyzed as a case study in the failure of appeasement as a deterrence strategy: each unopposed act of aggression lowered the perceived cost of the next, escalating from remilitarizing German soil, to annexing a German-speaking neighbor, to dismantling a third country's borders entirely. When evaluating any historical or contemporary instance of territorial coercion, ask what specific concession was granted, what strategic asset it delivered to the aggressor, and whether the counterparty's restraint reflected genuine deterrence or merely postponed confrontation. Within six months of Munich, Germany occupied the remainder of Czechoslovakia, exposing the agreement's core flaw: appeasement had not satisfied German ambitions but had instead signaled that further aggression would go unpunished, setting the stage for the invasion of Poland in 1939.
