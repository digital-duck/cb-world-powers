Germany 1917 Unrestricted Submarine Warfare

By early 1917, Germany's High Command faced a stalemate on the Western Front and a British naval blockade slowly strangling its economy. German strategists calculated that a policy of sinking all ships — neutral or belligerent, armed or not — approaching Britain without warning could cut off supplies and force Britain out of the war within five months. This was a calculated gamble: Germany knew unrestricted submarine warfare would likely draw the United States into the conflict, since American ships and citizens would inevitably be targeted, but German planners bet that Britain would collapse before American mobilization could matter on the battlefield.

The policy reversed an earlier German concession. After the 1915 sinking of the Lusitania killed over a hundred Americans and provoked outrage, Germany had scaled back submarine attacks to avoid antagonizing Washington. By 1917, however, military leaders judged that the war of attrition on land left no alternative but to escalate at sea, accepting American entry as the price of a faster victory.

The consequences arrived quickly. American merchant ships were sunk, and the interception of the Zimmermann Telegram — in which Germany proposed a military alliance with Mexico against the United States — further inflamed American public opinion. On April 6, 1917, the United States Congress declared war on Germany.

This decision illustrates a recurring pattern in the causal chain of escalation this book traces: a state facing battlefield deadlock adopts a high-risk policy whose short-term military logic (starving Britain of supplies) is weighed against a foreseeable long-term political cost (American entry), and the immediate pressure wins out. The same underlying dynamic appeared in the 1914 "blank check" that Germany gave Austria-Hungary — a commitment made to preserve military position and alliance credibility, in spite of clear risk of wider war. In both cases, decision-makers under strategic pressure discounted a probable but delayed consequence in favor of an urgent, calculable military advantage. Recognizing this pattern helps explain why rational actors repeatedly chose escalation over restraint throughout the war's unfolding.
