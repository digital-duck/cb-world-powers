Germany 1940 Blitzkrieg West

Blitzkrieg ("lightning war") was not a fixed doctrine written down in a manual before the fact — it was an emergent operational method that combined fast-moving armor, close air support, and radio-coordinated infantry to shatter an enemy's ability to organize a coherent defense, rather than to destroy it through attrition along a static front. The core idea: concentrate tanks and motorized troops at a narrow point (a Schwerpunkt, or "focal point"), punch through weakly held terrain, and then drive deep into the enemy's rear before reserves can react — bypassing strongpoints instead of reducing them one by one. Dive bombers like the Stuka substituted for slow-moving artillery, striking ahead of the spearhead to disrupt communications and morale. Speed itself became the weapon: an army defending along a continuous line assumes it has time to shift reserves to a threatened sector, and Blitzkrieg was designed specifically to deny that assumption.

Germany's campaign in the West illustrates this in two stages. In April 1940, Denmark fell in a day and Norway within weeks, securing Atlantic ports and iron-ore supply routes. Then in May, German forces struck the Low Countries as a deliberate feint — Allied armies rushed north into Belgium expecting a repeat of 1914's Schlieffen Plan — while the real armored thrust crossed the Ardennes Forest, terrain the French high command had judged nearly impassable to tanks and therefore left thinly defended. Panzer divisions under commanders like Guderian broke out at Sedan and raced to the English Channel in about ten days, encircling the bulk of the Allied field armies before those armies had even engaged the main German force. France capitulated by late June.

The strategic lesson generalizes beyond 1940: a defense built around a fixed perimeter is only as strong as its planners' assumptions about where the attack will come and how fast it can arrive. When you analyze any defended system — a fortified line, a network's security perimeter, a supply chain — ask not just "where is it strong?" but "where does the defender assume they have time to react, and what happens if that assumption is false?" Blitzkrieg's real innovation was exploiting tempo, not just firepower.
