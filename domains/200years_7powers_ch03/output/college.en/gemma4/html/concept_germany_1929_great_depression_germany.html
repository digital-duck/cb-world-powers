The period following the Wall Street Crash in October 1929 marked a catastrophic turning point for the Weimar Republic. The German economy, which had experienced relative stability and investment during the mid-1920s (often termed the "Golden Twenties"), was fundamentally dependent on foreign loans, particularly from the United States. When international credit markets seized up, the withdrawal of U.S. capital led to a severe economic contraction that transformed mass unemployment into profound political instability. This crisis illustrates how deep structural economic vulnerability can dismantle democratic institutions, regardless of initial political goodwill or treaty obligations.

Worked example: The collapse of external financing. Before 1929, German recovery was reliant on continuous streams of foreign investment and American loans (like those structured by the Dawes Plan). These funds propped up industrial activity and consumer spending. When global trade dried up and US banks recalled their capital—a process driven by domestic financial panic—the mechanisms supporting the Weimar economy failed. Unemployment soared dramatically, increasing from around 3 million in 1928 to over 6 million by 1932. This rapid economic decay was not merely a downturn; it was an absolute loss of faith in established capitalist and democratic systems, leading to civil unrest and industrial strikes that overwhelmed the state's capacity for crisis management.

Problem-solving application: The link between despair and radicalization. The core problem facing Weimar democracy was not simply economic hardship—it was the resulting political vacuum. When millions faced destitution, traditional political structures (like moderate socialist or centrist parties) failed to offer a clear path back to stability. Instead, voters became susceptible to extreme ideologies that promised simple solutions: total repudiation of treaties, national rebirth through violence, and scapegoating of internal or external enemies. The Great Depression thus acted as the primary accelerant for political radicalization. The economic collapse provided the necessary emotional fuel—fear, desperation, and humiliation—that allowed extremist groups, such as the Nazi Party (NSDAP), to gain unprecedented traction and dismantle the democratic process entirely.
