Usa 2021 January 6 Capitol Attack

On January 6, 2021, thousands of supporters of President Donald Trump gathered in Washington, D.C., after weeks of false claims that the 2020 presidential election had been stolen through fraud. As Congress convened in a joint session to certify Joe Biden's Electoral College victory — a step required by the Twelfth Amendment and the Electoral Count Act — a crowd breached security barriers and forced its way into the Capitol building. Lawmakers, staff, and Vice President Mike Pence were evacuated as rioters occupied the House and Senate chambers, vandalized offices, and clashed violently with law enforcement. Five people died in connection with the events, and more than a hundred police officers were injured. It was the first time since the War of 1812 that the Capitol had been overrun by hostile forces, and the first time in modern history that the peaceful transfer of presidential power was directly disrupted by force.

The certification resumed later that night once the building was secured, and Biden's victory was formally confirmed early on January 7. But the episode exposed how fragile long-standing democratic norms can be when political leaders actively contest a legitimate election outcome rather than concede. Unlike a coup carried out by military or state actors, this was a mass civilian action encouraged by rhetoric from the outgoing president and allied officials, making it a case study in how disinformation and institutional trust intersect with the mechanics of governance.
