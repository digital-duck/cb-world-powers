In November 2024, Donald Trump won a second presidential term, defeating Vice President Kamala Harris after President Biden withdrew from the race in July 2024. This event belongs to political science and public policy analysis, so the analytical tools here are conceptual frameworks—coalition dynamics, incumbency effects, and electoral mechanics—rather than mathematical formalism. Understanding it well means being able to trace why the race unfolded as it did, not just recite the outcome.

Three structural factors shaped the result. First, incumbency and the "referendum effect": when an unpopular incumbent (or their party) is on the ballot, elections often become a verdict on the status quo rather than a comparison of two visions. Biden's approval ratings had been low through 2023–2024, driven partly by inflation and immigration concerns; when Harris inherited the ticket, she inherited that referendum dynamic rather than escaping it. Second, the compressed-campaign problem: Biden's withdrawal left Harris roughly 100 days to build a national campaign, secure the nomination, and define herself to voters—far less time than a normal primary process affords. Third, the Electoral College's amplification of narrow state-level swings meant that modest shifts among working-class and suburban voters in a handful of battleground states (e.g., Pennsylvania, Georgia, Arizona) translated into a decisive electoral margin even where the national popular vote was close.
