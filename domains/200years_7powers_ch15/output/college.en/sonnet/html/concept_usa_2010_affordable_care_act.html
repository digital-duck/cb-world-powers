Usa 2010 Affordable Care Act

Definition. The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (ACA), signed by President Obama in March 2010, restructured the U.S. health insurance market through four interlocking mechanisms: (1) an individual mandate requiring most Americans to carry insurance or pay a penalty, (2) subsidized marketplace exchanges where individuals without employer coverage could buy private plans, (3) expansion of Medicaid eligibility to households up to 138% of the federal poverty line, and (4) consumer protections banning insurers from denying coverage or charging more for pre-existing conditions. The law emerged directly from the 2008 financial crisis: high unemployment had stripped millions of employer-sponsored coverage, exposing how tightly U.S. health insurance was tied to jobs, and a newly elected Democratic majority used that political window to pass reform that had failed under previous administrations.

Worked example. Consider the mandate's underlying economic logic: insurance markets fail when only sick people buy coverage, driving premiums up and pushing healthier people out — a spiral known as adverse selection. The ACA's designers reasoned that guaranteeing coverage for pre-existing conditions (a popular provision) would collapse the market unless healthy individuals were also required to enroll, spreading risk across the pool. This is why the mandate, subsidies, and consumer protections were bundled as a single package rather than passed separately — each depends on the others to keep premiums actuarially sound.

Problem-solving application. The ACA's legacy is contested precisely because its components can be selectively challenged. In NFIB v. Sebelius (2012), the Supreme Court upheld the mandate as a constitutional exercise of Congress's taxing power but ruled that Medicaid expansion could not be compulsory for states, splitting the country into expansion and non-expansion states with measurably different coverage rates. In 2017, Congress zeroed out the individual mandate penalty, testing whether the law could function without its original enforcement mechanism — a natural experiment political scientists and economists still study when evaluating how mandate-free insurance markets behave. Evaluating the ACA today means tracing how each surviving piece — exchanges, subsidies, partial Medicaid expansion — performs once decoupled from the design meant to hold them together.
