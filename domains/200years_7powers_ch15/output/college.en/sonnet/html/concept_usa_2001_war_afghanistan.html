Usa 2001 War Afghanistan

In the weeks after the September 11 attacks, the United States invoked the right of self-defense to justify military action against al-Qaeda and the Taliban regime that had sheltered it in Afghanistan. On October 7, 2001, US and coalition forces, working with the Afghan opposition group known as the Northern Alliance, launched Operation Enduring Freedom. Combining airstrikes with a small number of special operations troops and local ground fighters, the campaign toppled the Taliban government within weeks — Kabul fell by mid-November, and by December the Taliban had lost effective control of the country. This rapid initial success reflected a deliberate strategy: rather than deploying a large conventional invasion force, US planners paired airpower and intelligence assets with an existing indigenous fighting force that already opposed the Taliban.

Toppling a government, however, proved far easier than building a stable one to replace it. The US and its allies installed an interim administration under Hamid Karzai and committed to a long-term nation-building effort, including new security forces, elections, and infrastructure. But the Taliban regrouped, particularly from sanctuaries across the border in Pakistan, and by the mid-2000s the conflict had shifted into a classic counterinsurgency: an insurgent force blending into the civilian population, contesting rural territory, and using improvised explosive devices and ambushes against foreign troops and the Afghan government they backed. US troop levels fluctuated sharply over two decades — a major "surge" under the Obama administration peaked at roughly 100,000 troops around 2010–2011, followed by a gradual drawdown as the mission shifted toward training Afghan forces rather than direct combat.

The war's endpoint illustrates a recurring problem in counterinsurgency: military presence can suppress an insurgency without resolving the political conditions that sustain it. When the US signed a 2020 withdrawal agreement with the Taliban and completed troop removal in August 2021, the Afghan government and army — dependent on foreign support and lacking broad legitimacy — collapsed within days, and the Taliban retook Kabul almost without a fight. For students of policy and conflict, the case is a useful lens for weighing a core trade-off: short-term military victory versus the much harder, longer-term task of establishing a government that can survive on its own.
