Uk 2014 Scottish Independence Referendum

Definition. The 2014 Scottish independence referendum asked voters in Scotland a single yes/no question: "Should Scotland be an independent country?" It was held under the Edinburgh Agreement (2012), a formal deal between the UK government and the Scottish government that authorized the vote and bound both sides to respect the result. On September 18, 2014, Scots voted 55% "No" to 45% "Yes," with turnout at 84.6% — a record for any UK-wide or devolved election. Scotland remained part of the United Kingdom, but the campaign and its aftermath reshaped devolved politics rather than settling the underlying question of sovereignty.

Worked example. To see why the referendum happened when it did, connect it to $[[uk_2008_global_financial_crisis]]$. The 2008 crisis triggered bank bailouts, austerity budgets, and years of UK-wide spending cuts. In Scotland, this fed a narrative that Westminster's economic decisions were imposed without adequate Scottish consent, and that an independent Scotland could manage North Sea oil revenue and fiscal policy differently. The Scottish National Party (SNP), which had won a majority in the 2011 Scottish Parliament election partly on economic-discontent votes, used that mandate to demand — and secure — a legally binding referendum. The financial crisis didn't cause the vote by itself, but it supplied the economic grievance that made independence a mainstream rather than fringe position.

Problem-solving application. When analyzing any referendum result, separate three variables: the headline margin, the turnout, and the promises made to the losing side. Here, the 10-point margin looks decisive, but the near-record turnout signals the issue was far from settled as a matter of public engagement. Days before the vote, UK party leaders issued "The Vow," promising Scotland greater devolved powers (over taxation, welfare, and further fiscal autonomy) if it voted No — a commitment later partially fulfilled through the Scotland Act 2016. This is a recurring pattern in constitutional referendums: a close pre-vote poll often triggers last-minute concessions that outlast the vote itself and become new sources of dispute. Applying this framework helps explain why the 2016 Brexit vote — in which Scotland voted 62% to remain in the EU while the UK overall voted to leave — reopened the independence debate almost immediately, since it created exactly the kind of divergence between Scottish preferences and UK-wide outcomes that "The Vow" had been designed to defuse.
