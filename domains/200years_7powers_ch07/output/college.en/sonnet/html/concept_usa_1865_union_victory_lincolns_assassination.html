Usa 1865 Union Victory Lincolns Assassination

Definition. By early 1865, the Confederacy was militarily and economically exhausted. General Ulysses S. Grant's Overland Campaign and the fall of Richmond forced General Robert E. Lee to surrender the Army of Northern Virginia to Grant at Appomattox Courthouse, Virginia, on April 9, 1865. Lee's surrender did not legally end the war (scattered Confederate forces held out into May and June), but it marked the practical collapse of organized Southern resistance. Five days later, on April 14, 1865, John Wilkes Booth, a Confederate sympathizer, shot President Abraham Lincoln at Ford's Theatre; Lincoln died the next morning. In December 1865, the states ratified the 13th Amendment, which abolished slavery and involuntary servitude throughout the United States, closing the door the Emancipation Proclamation had only partially opened.

Worked example: connecting three events causally. The Emancipation Proclamation (1863) freed enslaved people only in Confederate-held territory and rested on Lincoln's wartime military authority — it could be reversed by a future court or Congress once the war ended. Union victory in 1865 removed the military obstacle to abolishing slavery everywhere, and Lincoln's assassination removed the president most identified with a moderate, reconciliation-focused Reconstruction. Congress had already passed the 13th Amendment in January 1865; state ratification through December 1865 converted emancipation from a wartime military order into a permanent constitutional guarantee, immune to future battlefield reversal. The amendment needed three-fourths of the states, including several former Confederate states readmitted under Reconstruction terms — itself a lever the federal government used to compel Southern acceptance.

Problem-solving application. Consider the historian's question: why did abolition require a constitutional amendment rather than resting on the Emancipation Proclamation alone? The answer lies in the legal architecture of the Proclamation: it was justified as a military necessity under the president's war powers, applied only to states in rebellion, and left slavery legally intact in border states like Kentucky and Delaware. A constitutional amendment closed both gaps — it applied nationwide and rested on the amendment process itself (Article V), not executive war powers, making it far harder to reverse. Students analyzing similar cases — for instance, whether an executive order or a statute better secures a policy against future political reversal — can apply the same reasoning: durability depends on the source of legal authority, not just the content of the rule.
