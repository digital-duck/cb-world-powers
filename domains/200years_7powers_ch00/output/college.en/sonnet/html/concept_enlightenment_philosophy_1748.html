Enlightenment Philosophy 1748

Before 1748, most European states were governed by a single will: a monarch who made the laws, enforced them, and judged disputes. Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws argued that this concentration of power is the root of tyranny, and that liberty requires different powers to be held by different hands. He identified three functions of government: the legislative power, which makes laws; the executive power, which carries them out and conducts foreign affairs; and the judicial power, which punishes crimes and settles disputes between individuals. Each should be exercised by a separate body, so that power checks power.

Rousseau, in The Social Contract (1762), asked a different question: where does legitimate authority come from? His answer was that laws bind people only when they express the general will, the common interest that citizens would agree to when thinking about the public good rather than private advantage. Government is a trustee of that will, not its owner, and it can be replaced if it stops serving it.

Together, these ideas give a practical test for any government.

Worked example. Suppose a national assembly passes a law making criticism of the government a crime. The same ministers who wrote the law then arrest and prosecute critics, before judges they appointed. Apply Montesquieu's framework in three steps. First, identify the functions: lawmaking, execution (arrest and prosecution), and adjudication. Second, check who holds each one. Here all three sit in one set of hands, so the separation has failed. Third, check for a check: can any body refuse the law or overturn a sentence? In this case, no. Montesquieu would call this tyranny regardless of how the ministers were chosen. Rousseau adds a second test: does the law express a general will, or only the interest of those who wrote it? A law that serves a faction fails that test even if a majority voted for it.

Problem-solving application. The same checklist works for any constitution or historical case. For each power, ask (1) what function it performs, (2) who appoints or removes its holders, and (3) what other power can block it. A system in which the executive can dismiss judges who rule against it has a weak separation, even if the branches have different names. This logic underlies the checks and balances in the American Constitution, and it is why French and Haitian revolutionaries invoked these ideas when they demanded representation and an end to arbitrary rule.
