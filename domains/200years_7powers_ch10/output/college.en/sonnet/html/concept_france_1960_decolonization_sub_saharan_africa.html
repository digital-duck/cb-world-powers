France 1960 Decolonization Sub Saharan Africa

Definition. In 1960, fourteen French colonies in Sub-Saharan Africa gained independence within a single calendar year — a moment often called the "Year of Africa." Senegal, Mali, Côte d'Ivoire, Niger, Upper Volta (now Burkina Faso), Dahomey (now Benin), Mauritania, Chad, Central African Republic, Congo-Brazzaville, Gabon, Madagascar, and others all transitioned from colonial administration to sovereign statehood. This was not a slow devolution but a coordinated, rapid dismantling of the second-largest colonial empire in Africa, made possible by the framework established two years earlier under [[france_1958_fifth_republic_established]], when Charles de Gaulle's new constitution offered colonies a choice between immediate independence or continued association within a "French Community." Most territories initially chose the Community, but by 1960 nearly all had renegotiated for full independence, and Paris — recognizing the shifting political tide, both domestically and across the decolonizing world — let them go with minimal resistance.

Worked example. Consider why France's approach differed sharply from other colonial powers' more contested exits — Algeria's brutal war of independence (1954–1962) being the counterexample within France's own empire. Sub-Saharan independence was comparatively negotiated rather than fought because France restructured the relationship rather than simply severing it: French remained the administrative and educational language, the CFA franc currency zone tied these new states' monetary policy to the French treasury, and defense pacts kept French military bases and advisors in place. This bundle of enduring ties became known as Françafrique — a term capturing how formal political sovereignty coexisted with continued economic and strategic dependency.

Problem-solving application. When evaluating any decolonization case, ask three questions this concept trains you to apply: (1) Was independence transferred through negotiation or won through armed conflict, and what does that predict about post-independence stability? (2) What structures — currency, language, military basing, trade preferences — persisted after the formal transfer of sovereignty, and whose interests did they serve? (3) How did the metropole's domestic political calculations (de Gaulle's need to end costly colonial wars and modernize France's global position) shape the timing and terms of exit? Applying this framework to 1960 explains both the speed of the transition and the durability of Françafrique as a live political and economic issue in Francophone Africa today.
