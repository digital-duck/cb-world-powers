France 2002 Euro Adopted

On January 1, 2002, the euro entered circulation as physical currency, and France formally withdrew the franc — a currency that had served the country, in one form or another, since Charlemagne's era — as legal tender. This event marked the completion of the transition that began with the [[france_1992_maastricht_treaty]], which had set the legal and economic criteria for monetary union nearly a decade earlier.

The 2002 cash changeover was the visible endpoint of a process that had actually started three years prior: the euro existed as an accounting currency from 1999, used for electronic transactions, stock listings, and interbank settlements, while francs remained the only cash in citizens' pockets. This staged rollout was itself a deliberate policy choice — it let banks, businesses, and payment systems adapt gradually before the far more disruptive step of replacing physical notes and coins overnight.

Consider the practical scale of the operation. France had to withdraw roughly 15 billion franc coins and banknotes from circulation while simultaneously distributing new euro cash to every bank, retailer, and ATM in the country — all within a matter of weeks. Prices had to be displayed in both currencies during a transition period so consumers could adjust to the new scale of value (roughly 6.56 francs to 1 euro, the fixed conversion rate locked in back in 1999). This is a logistics and coordination problem as much as a monetary one: any single country acting alone could not simply "decide" to adopt a shared currency — it required synchronized action across all participating states, since a common currency only functions if it is common everywhere at once.

The deeper significance for problem-solving in political economy is this: adopting a shared currency permanently forfeits an independent monetary policy. France could no longer devalue the franc to boost exports or set its own interest rates to fight inflation — those levers moved to the European Central Bank, whose decisions must serve the entire eurozone rather than any single member. When evaluating why later crises (such as the 2010s eurozone debt crisis) hit some member states harder than others, this loss of individual monetary flexibility is a central factor: countries in the eurozone can no longer use currency devaluation as a shock absorber for local economic downturns, unlike nations that retain their own currency.
