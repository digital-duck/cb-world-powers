France 2024 Snap Election Hung Parliament

Following heavy losses to Marine Le Pen's National Rally in the June 2024 European Parliament elections, President Emmanuel Macron dissolved the National Assembly and called a snap legislative election—a constitutional prerogative under Article 12 of the Fifth Republic. The gamble was to force voters to choose between his centrist bloc and the far right before dissatisfaction hardened further, but it produced the opposite of a mandate: a three-way split among the left-wing New Popular Front (NFP), Macron's Ensemble coalition, and the National Rally, with no bloc anywhere near the 289 seats needed for a majority. France entered a "hung parliament"—a legislature in which no single party or pre-formed coalition can govern alone.

A hung parliament is not a technical malfunction; it is a structural outcome of proportional or multi-round systems where three or more viable blocs compete. France's two-round majoritarian voting (a candidate needs an absolute majority in round one, or a plurality in round two after weaker candidates withdraw) usually manufactures a governing majority even from a fragmented electorate, because parties strategically stand down candidates to block rivals. In 2024, left and centrist candidates repeatedly withdrew to unite against the National Rally, which suppressed its seat count but also fragmented the "winning" side into mutually hostile factions—so the anti-RN alliance that worked at the ballot box could not translate into a governing coalition afterward.

The practical problem this creates is one of coalition arithmetic: a prime minister must survive a vote of no confidence, meaning any government needs either an outright majority or the tacit toleration of enough opposition deputies to abstain rather than topple it. With NFP, Ensemble, and RN each too large to ignore and too opposed to formally combine, Macron's appointees (first Gabriel Attal's caretaker government, then Michel Barnier) had to govern through ad hoc, issue-by-issue deals, budget concessions, and abstention-buying rather than a stable programmatic majority. This dynamic—legitimate election, fragmented result, ungovernable majority math—illustrates a recurring failure mode of snap elections called to "clarify" a political crisis: the tool designed to resolve instability can instead formalize it, leaving the underlying disagreement unresolved but now embedded in the institutions meant to manage it.
