France 1992 Maastricht Treaty

The Maastricht Treaty, signed in February 1992 and submitted to a French referendum that September, transformed the European Economic Community into the European Union. It created a three-pillar structure — the existing economic community, a common foreign and security policy, and cooperation on justice and home affairs — and laid out the roadmap for a single European currency, the euro, with convergence criteria on inflation, debt, and deficits that member states would have to meet before adopting it.

For France, ratification was not a formality. President François Mitterrand, who had built closer Franco-German ties throughout the 1980s, called a referendum rather than routing approval solely through parliament, gambling that popular endorsement would give the treaty greater legitimacy. The vote, held on September 20, 1992, produced one of the narrowest outcomes in French referendum history: 51.05% in favor, 48.95% against — a margin so thin the result became known as the "petit oui" (small yes). Turnout was high, and the closeness of the vote exposed a genuine split in French public opinion between supporters who saw European integration as consolidating peace and prosperity, and skeptics who worried about loss of national sovereignty, especially over monetary policy and the eventual abandonment of the franc.

This episode illustrates a recurring problem in analyzing referenda and treaty ratification: a razor-thin majority is legally decisive but politically fragile. A 51–49 outcome authorizes the treaty exactly as fully as a 90–10 outcome would, yet it signals a nearly evenly divided electorate whose unresolved half can resurface. Students of political analysis should treat referendum margins as a signal distinct from the binary outcome itself — the "small yes" of 1992 foreshadowed later French votes, including the 2005 rejection of the EU constitutional treaty, where accumulated skepticism about deeper integration finally prevailed. When evaluating any ratification event, the practical exercise is to ask two separate questions: did the measure pass, and how much durable political capital did passing it actually cost? Maastricht answers the first question decisively and the second one ambiguously, which is precisely why its aftermath continued to shape French and European politics for over a decade.
