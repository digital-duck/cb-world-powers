Russia 1936 Great Purge

The Great Purge (1936–1938) was Joseph Stalin's campaign of mass political repression, in which real and imagined rivals — Communist Party officials, military officers, intellectuals, and ordinary citizens denounced by neighbors or coerced informants — were arrested, executed, or sent to the Gulag, the Soviet system of forced-labor camps. Unlike a single purge trial, it was a sustained wave of terror enforced by the NKVD (secret police), operating through show trials, quota-based arrest targets, and networks of forced confession.

The clearest case study is the decapitation of the Red Army officer corps. Between 1937 and 1938, roughly three of five marshals, most army-corps commanders, and thousands of colonels and generals were executed or imprisoned — including Marshal Mikhail Tukhachevsky, one of the Soviet Union's most capable strategic thinkers. This was not incidental collateral damage; it was Stalin's deliberate mechanism for eliminating any officer whose loyalty he doubted, regardless of military competence. The purge therefore illustrates a recurring feature of authoritarian consolidation: a regime will sometimes trade institutional capability for the certainty of personal control, even when that trade carries severe strategic cost.

That cost became visible almost immediately. When Germany invaded in June 1941, the Red Army's command structure was staffed disproportionately by inexperienced replacements, and early Soviet defeats were compounded by a leadership vacuum the purge itself had created. This is the analytical link worth practicing: connect a domestic political decision (eliminating perceived threats to Stalin's authority) to a downstream strategic outcome (military unpreparedness in 1941) via a specific causal mechanism — the removal of experienced commanders — rather than treating the two events as merely sequential.

Note the compositional relationship to the Holodomor (1932–33): both episodes stem from the same underlying logic of centralized control enforced through terror, first against the peasantry to break resistance to collectivization, then against the party and military elite to eliminate any internal check on Stalin's power. When analyzing authoritarian regimes, look for this pattern — repression tends to escalate from external populations toward the regime's own institutions once initial resistance is suppressed, because the tools and precedents of terror, once normalized, are redirected inward as the leader's threat perception widens.
