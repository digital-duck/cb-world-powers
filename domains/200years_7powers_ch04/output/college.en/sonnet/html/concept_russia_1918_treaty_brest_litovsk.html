Russia 1918 Treaty Brest Litovsk

Definition. The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, signed March 3, 1918, ended Russia's participation in World War I. Facing a collapsing army, mass desertion, and a fragile new Bolshevik government struggling to survive its first months in power, Vladimir Lenin's regime accepted Germany's terms rather than continue fighting. Those terms were punishing: Russia surrendered Poland, Finland, the Baltic states, Ukraine, and parts of the Caucasus — territory containing roughly a third of its population, half its industrial base, and most of its coal production. The treaty exemplifies a recurring pattern in the study of revolutions and civil conflict: a government's external commitments (continuing a war) are frequently sacrificed to secure its internal survival (consolidating domestic power).

Worked example: modeling the trade-off. Treat this as a resource-allocation problem. Lenin's government had finite military and administrative capacity, $C$, that had to be split between two fronts: fighting Germany externally, $x$, and suppressing internal opposition (the emerging White armies and regional separatists) building toward the Russian Civil War, $C - x$. Continuing the war against Germany required $x$ large enough to hold the front — realistically close to the full pre-revolutionary military effort. But the Bolsheviks had already gutted army discipline through their own propaganda and needed troops, rail capacity, and political attention for the civil war they knew was coming. Setting $x$ near zero (exiting the war entirely) freed nearly all of $C$ for internal consolidation, at the cost of the territorial and economic concessions described above. Brest-Litovsk is the Bolsheviks choosing $x \approx 0$: they judged that losing Ukraine's grain and the Baltic ports was more survivable than losing the civil war.

Problem-solving application. This trade-off framework generalizes to any revolutionary regime deciding whether to honor a costly prior commitment. Ask three questions when analyzing such a decision: (1) What is the cost of continuing the commitment, measured in the resource most scarce to the regime (here, soldiers and political legitimacy)? (2) What is the cost of abandoning it, measured in a different currency (here, territory and economic capacity)? (3) Which scarce resource is closer to zero — and therefore more urgent — at the decision point? Applying this to Brest-Litovsk explains why the treaty, though it looks like capitulation, was a rational move given that manpower and political survival, not territory, were the Bolsheviks' binding constraint in early 1918.
