Russia 1917 Russian Revolution

By 1917, Russia's war effort had collapsed the state's legitimacy. Three years of catastrophic losses against Germany—millions of casualties, food and fuel shortages in the cities, and a government widely seen as incompetent and corrupt—produced two revolutions within a single year, each toppling a different regime.

February 1917 began as a bread shortage and factory strikes in Petrograd. When soldiers garrisoned in the capital refused orders to fire on demonstrators, the mutiny spread and Tsar Nicholas II lost control of his own army. Facing abandonment by his generals, he abdicated within days, ending three centuries of Romanov rule. Power passed to a Provisional Government, formed from liberal and moderate members of the old parliament (the Duma), but it shared authority uneasily with the Petrograd Soviet, a council of workers' and soldiers' deputies—an arrangement historians call "dual power." The Provisional Government's fatal decision was to keep Russia in World War I, a war the population desperately wanted ended.

October 1917 was a far more targeted event. Vladimir Lenin's Bolshevik Party, a disciplined Marxist faction that had gained strength in the soviets throughout the year, judged the Provisional Government too weak to resist. Under the slogan "Peace, Land, and Bread," the Bolsheviks organized armed workers and soldiers (Red Guards) to seize key installations in Petrograd—the telegraph office, train stations, the Winter Palace—with minimal violence, since the government had almost no loyal troops left to defend it.

To apply this as an analytical case rather than a narrative to memorize, treat 1917 as a two-stage causal chain: (1) war strain removes the old regime's coercive and economic capacity, creating a legitimacy vacuum; (2) an organized minority with a clear, resonant program exploits that vacuum before a fragmented majority can consolidate power. This model explains why the Bolsheviks—a small party—succeeded where the larger, more popular Provisional Government failed: the latter tried to govern through existing institutions while continuing an unpopular war, while the former offered an immediate, unambiguous resolution to the population's core grievances. This same pattern—institutional collapse followed by seizure by a disciplined minority—recurs in other revolutionary case studies and is a useful lens for comparing 1917 to later 20th-century upheavals.
