Russia 1932 Holodomor

Definition. The Holodomor ("death by hunger") was a famine that struck Soviet Ukraine and the ethnically Ukrainian Kuban region in 1932–1933, killing an estimated 3–5 million people. It was not primarily a crop failure but the direct outcome of Stalin's forced collectivization, launched under the First Five-Year Plan. When peasants resisted surrendering their land, livestock, and grain to state-run collective farms, the government responded with escalating grain requisition quotas, set so high that villages were left with nothing to eat. Brigades searched homes and confiscated even seed grain and buried food reserves. Ukraine's internal borders were sealed, trapping starving peasants inside the famine zone and preventing them from traveling to buy bread elsewhere. Because the underlying cause was policy rather than weather or blight, many historians and several governments classify the Holodomor as a genocide — a famine engineered, in part, to break Ukrainian peasant resistance and national identity.

Worked example. Consider a village required to deliver 70% of its harvest as quota, up from a historical norm of roughly 25–30%. If the harvest yields enough calories to feed the village for twelve months at full quota compliance, delivering 70% leaves only about 30% of that food — roughly 3.6 months' worth stretched, in reality, across a full year. Multiply this shortfall across thousands of villages, and the arithmetic of the quota system alone predicts mass starvation, independent of any drought.

Problem-solving application. Historians and demographers reconstruct famine mortality using indirect methods rather than direct death counts, since Soviet authorities suppressed 1930s census data. One key technique is a demographic balancing equation:

$$ D = P_1 - P_2 - B + \text{(net migration)} $$

where $P_1$ and $P_2$ are population totals from two censuses, $B$ is expected births, and $D$ is excess deaths. Applying this to Ukraine's 1926 and 1939 census figures, adjusted for expected natural growth and known migration, yields the 3–5 million estimate. This exercise illustrates a broader skill: quantifying mass mortality events where direct records are censored or destroyed, a method also used to estimate deaths in other twentieth-century famines and atrocities.
