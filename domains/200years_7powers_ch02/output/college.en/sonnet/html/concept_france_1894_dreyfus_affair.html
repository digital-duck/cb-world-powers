France 1894 Dreyfus Affair

Definition. In 1894, French military intelligence discovered that classified documents were being passed to the German embassy in Paris. Suspicion fell on Captain Alfred Dreyfus, one of the few Jewish officers on the French General Staff, based largely on a handwriting comparison that later proved unreliable. A secret court-martial convicted him of treason; he was stripped of rank in a public ceremony and deported to Devil's Island in French Guiana. The case became a national crisis not because the verdict was unusual, but because evidence soon surfaced — and was actively suppressed by army officials — showing that the real culprit was another officer, Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy. Rather than admit error, the military command fabricated additional documents to shore up the original conviction, turning a flawed prosecution into an institutional cover-up.

Worked example. The affair split France into two camps: "Dreyfusards," who demanded a retrial in the name of justice and civil liberties, and "anti-Dreyfusards," who saw the case as a matter of military authority and national honor, often laced with open antisemitism. The turning point came in January 1898, when novelist Émile Zola published "J'Accuse...!," an open letter in the newspaper L'Aurore accusing the army of deliberate wrongdoing and identifying specific officers responsible for the frame-up. Zola was convicted of libel and fled to England, but his letter forced the case into the center of public and international debate, making it impossible for the government to treat the matter as a closed military affair.

Problem-solving application. Evaluating the affair requires separating three distinct failures: an evidentiary error (misread handwriting), an institutional failure (the army's refusal to correct course once new evidence emerged), and a societal failure (the willingness of much of the public and press to accept a scapegoat along ethnic lines). Dreyfus was retried in 1899, pardoned, but not formally exonerated until 1906, when a civilian court of appeals overturned the conviction entirely and reinstated him in the army. The case is a foundational example, still used in discussions of judicial and military accountability, of how institutions can prioritize protecting their own credibility over correcting a known injustice — and of how a free press and organized public dissent can eventually force that correction.
