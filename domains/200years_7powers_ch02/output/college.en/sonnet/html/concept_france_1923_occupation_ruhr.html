France 1923 Occupation Ruhr

Definition. In January 1923, France and Belgium sent troops into the Ruhr Valley, Germany's industrial heartland, after the Weimar government defaulted on coal and timber deliveries owed under the Treaty of Versailles reparations schedule. The stated goal was direct extraction: if Germany would not pay, the Allies would seize the resources themselves by operating Ruhr mines, railways, and factories under military control. France's foreign minister Raymond Poincaré framed the move as enforcement of a legal obligation, not aggression.

How it unfolded. The German government responded with "passive resistance" — ordering Ruhr workers, civil servants, and factory managers to halt cooperation and refuse to work for the occupiers. Berlin financed this resistance by printing money to pay striking workers and idle officials, at a moment when the German mark was already weakened by wartime debt and prior reparations strain. The result was catastrophic: German industrial output collapsed, the occupation yielded almost none of the resources France had hoped to extract, and the flood of new currency detonated the hyperinflation of 1923, in which prices doubled every few days and the mark's exchange value fell from roughly 4.2 to the dollar in 1914 to over 4 trillion to the dollar by late 1923. Savings, pensions, and fixed incomes across Germany were wiped out.

Problem-solving application. The episode is a case study in how enforcement mechanisms can backfire when they ignore economic interdependence. France treated reparations as a straightforward collection problem — apply force, extract value — but the Ruhr's output depended on a functioning currency, willing labor, and intact supply chains, all of which the occupation and the resistance it provoked destroyed. Analyzing this failure means asking: what is the actual capacity constraint on the debtor, and does the coercive action target that constraint or an unrelated variable? France targeted physical assets (mines, rail lines) while the real bottleneck was monetary stability and political will; the mismatch produced negative returns for the creditor and a radicalized, embittered debtor population. The 1924 Dawes Plan, which restructured reparations around Germany's actual payment capacity and tied it to American loans, was a direct corrective response — and the diplomatic scar tissue from 1923 shaped French anxiety and German resentment through the interwar period, feeding into [[france_1919_treaty_versailles]]'s unresolved reparations question and the broader collapse of Franco-German trust before 1939.
