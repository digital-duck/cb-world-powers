France 1914 World War I

France entered World War I in August 1914 following the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand and the collapse of Europe's alliance system into general war. As the primary Western Front battleground, France absorbed the war's heaviest continuous fighting for four years, ending with roughly 1.4 million French dead — over 4% of the pre-war population, and a still-larger share of men of military age.

The defining episode was the Battle of Verdun (February–December 1916). German strategy, articulated by Chief of Staff Erich von Falkenhayn, deliberately targeted a symbolically vital French fortress city not to capture ground but to "bleed France white" — inflicting unsustainable casualties through attrition rather than maneuver. The battle produced roughly 700,000 combined casualties over ten months for a front line that barely moved. Verdun illustrates trench warfare's core tactical problem: industrial-age weapons (machine guns, rapid-fire artillery, barbed wire) gave defenders overwhelming advantage over attackers crossing open ground, so both sides could inflict massive losses on each other while neither could achieve a breakthrough.

Applying this to the war's broader logic: attrition warfare works only when one side's capacity to replace losses (in soldiers, matériel, and morale) outlasts the other's. Analyze Verdun as a resource-exhaustion contest rather than a battle over territory. France defended the fortress at catastrophic cost specifically because losing it was judged politically and symbolically unaffordable — a hostage strategy Falkenhayn correctly bet on, even though Germany suffered nearly as many casualties as France in the process, arguably undermining the plan's own logic.

This mutual exhaustion connects Verdun to why the war ultimately required outside intervention (the entry of the United States in 1917) to break the stalemate, and why the postwar peace settlement was shaped by French demands for security guarantees against a resurgent Germany — demands rooted directly in the trauma of having absorbed the Western Front's casualties on home soil. Understanding France's 1914–1918 experience is thus essential context for interpreting both the harshness of the Treaty of Versailles and France's defensive military posture (culminating later in the Maginot Line) in the interwar period.
