France 1884 Berlin Conference Scramble Africa

Definition. The Berlin Conference (November 1884–February 1885) was a diplomatic meeting of fourteen European powers, plus the United States and the Ottoman Empire, convened in Berlin at the invitation of German Chancellor Otto von Bismarck to regulate European colonization and trade in Africa. No African representatives were present. The conference produced the General Act of Berlin, which established rules for the continent's partition: a claim to African territory had to be backed by "effective occupation" (actual administrative or military presence, not merely a flag or treaty on paper) to be recognized by other powers, and it guaranteed free navigation on the Congo and Niger Rivers. France, already the dominant power in North and West Africa after its 1881 protectorate over Tunisia, used the conference to legitimize and accelerate its ambitions across West and Central Africa.

Worked example. Consider how "effective occupation" reshaped French strategy after 1885. Rather than negotiating isolated treaties, French officers such as Louis Faidherbe's successors pursued a deliberate east-west corridor strategy, sending military columns from Senegal toward Lake Chad and the Niger Bend, planting garrisons and signing chieftaincy treaties along the way to satisfy the occupation requirement. This produced French West Africa (Afrique Occidentale Française) and French Equatorial Africa (Afrique Équatoriale Française), federations eventually spanning territories including modern Senegal, Mali, Niger, Chad, Gabon, and the Republic of Congo.

Problem-solving application. Historians use the Berlin Conference as a case study in institutional design: a rule intended to reduce inter-European conflict (effective occupation) had the unintended consequence of accelerating conquest, since powers now had incentive to occupy territory quickly rather than negotiate slowly. When analyzing a colonial claim from this period, ask three diagnostic questions: (1) What treaty or administrative act formalized the claim? (2) What military or administrative presence backed it within a reasonable timeframe? (3) How did rival powers' competing claims in the same region get arbitrated—by further negotiation, by force, or by border-drawing that ignored existing African political or ethnic boundaries? Applying this framework to France's post-1884 expansion explains both its rapid territorial growth and the artificial, often ethnically discontinuous borders that persist across West and Central Africa today—a direct, traceable consequence of a rule written by outsiders with no African input.
