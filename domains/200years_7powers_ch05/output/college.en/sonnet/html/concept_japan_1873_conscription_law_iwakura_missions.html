Japan 1873 Conscription Law Iwakura Missions

The Conscription Ordinance of January 1873 made military service a universal male obligation in Japan, replacing the samurai's hereditary monopoly on arms with a citizen army drawn from the entire population. Every male subject reaching age twenty became liable for three years of active duty followed by reserve obligations, regardless of former caste status. This measure followed directly from the 1871 abolition of the han system: once the domains and their private retainer bands were dissolved, the new Meiji state needed a national force loyal to the central government rather than to regional lords, and conscription supplied it.

The law drew heavily on foreign models. War Minister Yamagata Aritomo studied French and Prussian conscription systems, and the ordinance's architecture — universal registration, standardized terms of service, exemption clauses for eldest sons, students, and certain fee-payers — mirrored European practice while adapting it to Japan's fiscal and administrative capacity. The exemptions mattered in practice: roughly the wealthier and better-connected households could purchase or study their way out of service, so the immediate burden fell disproportionately on poorer farming families, sparking scattered rural protests (the "blood tax riots," so named because peasants misread the term ketsuzei, "blood tax," literally).

The same year, the Iwakura Mission returned to Japan after nearly two years touring the United States and Europe (1871–1873). Led by Iwakura Tomomi and including future architects of Meiji policy such as Okubo Toshimichi and Ito Hirobumi, the mission had originally sought treaty revision to end the unequal treaties imposed by Western powers; it failed at that goal but succeeded at a deeper one — its members returned convinced that Japan's industrial base, legal codes, and educational and military institutions all required systematic overhaul before Japan could negotiate with the West as an equal.

Problem-solving application: to assess why 1873 marks an inflection point rather than an isolated policy change, compare institutional inputs and outputs across the 1871–1873 window — han abolition removed 261 domains' private armies; conscription created a single national force under the War Ministry; the Iwakura Mission's findings redirected government priority toward the Ministry of Industry and later the 1889 Constitution. Students should trace how removing one institution (feudal military structure) created the administrative necessity that a second institution (universal conscription) then filled — a pattern of sequential dependency that recurs throughout the Meiji reforms of the 1870s–1880s.
