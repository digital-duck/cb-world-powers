The global economic shockwave originating in the United States following the 1929 stock market crash proved devastating for Japan, which had structured its industrial and rural economy around international trade. By 1930, collapsing demand for key Japanese exports—particularly silk textiles—decimated the country’s export revenue. This crisis was fundamentally an issue of external economic dependency: when global markets seized up, countless rural economies could not absorb the shock. The resulting widespread unemployment and poverty in agricultural regions did not simply lead to hardship; it created a fertile ground for political radicalization. The economic failure fostered deep distrust, leading Japanese citizens to blame non-military authorities—specifically career politicians and established business elites—for failing to protect Japan's national interests during the downturn.

Consider the collapse of the silk industry as a worked example of this mechanism. Before the Depression, Japan’s textile exports were crucial sources of foreign currency. However, when global consumer spending plummeted, prices for Japanese silks fell precipitously. A single factory that previously processed $10 million in annual revenue might see its input value drop by 40% within two years. This immediate and severe loss translated directly from the urban industrial centers to the rural hinterlands. As textile workers lost jobs and cash-poor farmers could not sell their goods, social stability frayed. The economic problem thus became a political one: who was responsible for the failure?

The resulting application of this crisis mechanism saw opposition consolidate around militarist groups. These groups offered a seemingly simple solution to complex economic problems: self-sufficiency (autarky) achieved through military expansion and resource acquisition abroad. Instead of relying on volatile global markets, they argued Japan must secure its own resources by force—a policy that fundamentally rejected the existing international order represented by treaties and liberal capitalism. The Great Depression thus provided the critical political fuel for ultranationalism; the economic failure was successfully transformed into a nationalistic mandate demanding aggressive foreign action to guarantee survival.
