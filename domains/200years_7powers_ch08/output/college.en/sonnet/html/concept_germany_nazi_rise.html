Germany Nazi Rise

Weimar Germany's instability — hyperinflation, political fragmentation, and the trauma of the Depression — created the conditions the Nazi Party exploited to seize and consolidate power in a matter of weeks. Appointed Chancellor on January 30, 1933, Adolf Hitler headed a coalition cabinet in which conservative elites assumed they could control him. That assumption collapsed almost immediately. On February 27, 1933, the Reichstag building burned; the Nazis blamed a communist conspiracy and used the fire to justify the Reichstag Fire Decree, suspending civil liberties, habeas corpus, and freedom of the press. Weeks later, the Enabling Act of March 23, 1933, passed with the two-thirds majority needed to amend the constitution — secured through the exclusion of arrested communist deputies and pressure on the Catholic Center Party — let Hitler's cabinet enact laws without Reichstag approval or presidential consent. This single act dismantled the separation of powers that had defined Weimar democracy, converting a chancellorship into a legal dictatorship.

State antisemitism escalated in step with this consolidation of power. The 1935 Nuremberg Laws stripped Jews of German citizenship and criminalized marriage or sexual relations between Jews and "Aryans," codifying racial persecution into statute. Kristallnacht, the November 1938 pogrom, marked a shift from legal discrimination to open state-sanctioned violence: synagogues burned, Jewish businesses were destroyed, and thousands were arrested, with police and fire services ordered not to intervene. This trajectory culminated at the Wannsee Conference of January 1942, where senior officials coordinated the logistics of what they termed the "Final Solution" — the systematic deportation and murder of European Jews.

To evaluate this history as an interpretive problem rather than a fixed narrative, consider the mechanism, not just the sequence: legal instruments (decrees, an enabling statute) converted a crisis (the fire) into permanent authority, while each subsequent escalation in persecution relied on prior steps having gone unchallenged. Analyzing primary sources — the text of the Enabling Act, contemporary newspaper coverage of Kristallnacht, or the Wannsee Protocol itself — reveals how bureaucratic and legal language obscured the radicalism of what was being authorized, a pattern worth testing against other historical transitions from democratic norms to authoritarian rule.
