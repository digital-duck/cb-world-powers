Russia Wwi Collapse

By 1917, three years of world war had detonated the structural weaknesses Russia had barely papered over since 1905. The autocracy entered the conflict in 1914 to honor its alliance with Serbia against Austria-Hungary and Germany — part of the great-power rivalry that had locked Europe's empires into rigid alliance blocs. Russia's army, though numerically vast, was chronically outmatched: poorly supplied, thinly equipped with modern artillery and rifles, and led by officers whose command structures had not meaningfully modernized. The result was a string of catastrophic defeats. At Tannenberg in August 1914, German forces encircled and destroyed the Russian Second Army, inflicting roughly 170,000 casualties in a single battle. Subsequent campaigns bled the army further, and by 1916 Russia had suffered millions of dead, wounded, and captured soldiers — losses no economy of that era could absorb without consequence.

The war's demands collided directly with the unresolved grievances of 1905. Conscription stripped the countryside of labor precisely when agricultural output was needed most, while the railway network — overwhelmed by military logistics — failed to move what grain existed to the cities. Bread shortages and fuel scarcity became routine in Petrograd by the winter of 1916–1917. Inflation eroded wages, and industrial workers, already organized through the soviets that had emerged in 1905, had both the grievance and the institutional memory to act again. When bread riots broke out in Petrograd in late February 1917, the garrison troops ordered to suppress them mutinied instead, joining the protesters rather than firing on them — a decisive break in the coercive apparatus the Tsar had relied on twelve years earlier.

Stripped of military credibility and now lacking even the loyalty of his own troops, Tsar Nicholas II abdicated on March 2, 1917 (February 27 by the old Julian calendar Russia still used), ending three centuries of Romanov rule. The collapse illustrates a general pattern in the study of state breakdown: external shocks like war do not usually create instability from nothing — they accelerate and compound crises a regime has already failed to resolve. The 1905 crisis had exposed the fault lines; World War I supplied the pressure that fractured them completely.
