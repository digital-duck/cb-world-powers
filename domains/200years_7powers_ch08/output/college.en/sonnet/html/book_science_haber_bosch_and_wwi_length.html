Science Haber-Bosch and WWI Length is the point where the two chains this book has traced — the science of industrial chemistry and the political-military history of Germany — physically intersect in a single fact: the same laboratory discovery that fed the world also let a blockaded nation keep fighting. Fritz Haber's 1909 synthesis of ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen, scaled to industrial output by Carl Bosch at BASF's Oppau plant, was developed to solve an agricultural problem — the looming exhaustion of Chile's natural nitrate deposits, the world's main source of fertilizer nitrogen. But nitrates are also the feedstock for nitric acid, and nitric acid is the feedstock for explosives. When the Royal Navy imposed its blockade on Germany in August 1914, it cut off Chilean nitrate imports almost entirely — Germany had been importing over 900,000 tons annually before the war. Without a substitute, German war planners' own prewar estimates suggested munitions production would collapse within roughly six months. Instead, Oppau and the wartime Leuna works synthesized nitrogen compounds domestically, and Germany fought for four more years.

This is why the concept sits where it does: it draws directly on Germany's WWI trajectory (the blockade, the demand for sustained war production) and on Haber's parallel scientific legacy in chemical weapons and aviation-related chemistry, showing one scientist's work branching into both an economic lifeline and an instrument of battlefield atrocity — Haber also directed the first large-scale chlorine gas attack at Ypres in 1915. Read together, the two source concepts converge on a single, concrete historical fact: industrial chemistry did not merely support the war passively, it altered its expected length by removing a resource constraint that Germany's enemies had counted on being fatal.
