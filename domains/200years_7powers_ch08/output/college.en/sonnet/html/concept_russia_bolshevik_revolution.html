Russia Bolshevik Revolution

By autumn 1917, Russia's Provisional Government had squandered the credibility it inherited from the February Revolution. It kept Russia in World War I despite catastrophic losses, failed to distribute land to peasants, and could not feed cities wracked by strikes. Vladimir Lenin's Bolshevik Party exploited this vacuum with a program of stark simplicity: "Peace, Land, Bread." On the night of October 24–25, 1917 (Julian calendar), Bolshevik-led Red Guards seized railway stations, telegraph offices, and the Winter Palace in Petrograd with almost no bloodshed — less a mass uprising than a disciplined coup executed against a government with no remaining will to resist.

Holding power proved far harder than taking it. From 1918 to 1921, the Bolsheviks (Reds) fought a multi-front Civil War against a fractured coalition of monarchists, liberals, and rival socialists (Whites), complicated by foreign intervention from Britain, France, the United States, and Japan. The Reds won through central control of the rail network connecting major cities, unified command under Leon Trotsky's Red Army, and "War Communism" — the forced requisitioning of grain and nationalization of industry needed to supply the front. This policy kept the army fed but devastated the peasant economy and triggered widespread famine.

The costs of War Communism became politically explosive by 1921. Sailors at the Kronstadt naval base — once staunch Bolshevik supporters — rebelled, demanding free elections and an end to grain requisitioning. The revolt was crushed by force within weeks, but it revealed how dangerously isolated the regime had become even from its own base. Lenin's response was the New Economic Policy (NEP): a tactical retreat that replaced requisitioning with a fixed tax, permitted small-scale private trade and farming, while the state retained control of banking, heavy industry, and foreign trade.

This case illustrates a recurring problem in revolutionary state-building: seizing power and governing durably require different capacities entirely. A regime that wins a civil war by extracting maximum resources from the population may need to reverse that extraction to survive in peacetime. NEP was that reversal — an ideological compromise accepted precisely because Kronstadt showed the alternative was collapse. On these consolidated foundations, the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was formally proclaimed in December 1922.
