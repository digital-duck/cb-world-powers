Russia Stalinist Terror

By 1928, Stalin had consolidated power and abandoned Lenin's New Economic Policy, which had permitted peasants to sell surplus grain on the open market. In its place came forced collectivization: private farms were merged into state-controlled kolkhozes, and grain quotas were set not by what the land could sustainably yield but by what the state demanded — chiefly to fund rapid industrialization under the First Five-Year Plan. Peasants who resisted, especially the better-off "kulaks," were deported, imprisoned, or shot. The result, concentrated in Ukraine and the North Caucasus, was the Holodomor of 1932–33: a famine caused not by drought but by policy, as requisition brigades stripped villages of grain even as people starved. Death toll estimates range from three to seven million.

Collectivization's economic logic reveals why the famine was man-made rather than natural. Soviet planners needed hard currency and capital goods from abroad, and grain exports were the primary lever available. If a village's harvest was $H$ tons but the state quota was set at $Q > H - S$, where $S$ is the subsistence grain needed to keep the population alive, then meeting the quota mathematically guaranteed starvation. Officials, however, treated $Q$ as fixed and treated reports of shortfall as sabotage — a political rather than agronomic explanation. This logic matters for understanding the era: Stalinist terror did not stem from isolated cruelty but from a system where inconvenient data was reinterpreted as disloyalty, a pattern that recurred at even greater scale in the Great Purge of 1936–38.

The Purge extended this same reasoning to the party and military. Show trials eliminated Old Bolsheviks on fabricated charges, and roughly half the Red Army's officer corps — including three of five marshals and most senior generals — was executed or imprisoned. Applying this to historical analysis: when Germany invaded in June 1941, the Soviet military lacked experienced command precisely because Stalin had spent the prior three years destroying it. Students should connect cause and effect here — the same apparatus that manufactured famine through unrealistic quotas manufactured military catastrophe through unfounded purges, showing how ideological control mechanisms, once built, tend to consume the institutions meant to serve them.
