France Colonial Expansion

Building on the Third Republic's drive to compensate for defeat in the Franco-Prussian War with overseas prestige, French colonial expansion in the 1880s and 1890s converted scattered trading posts and missionary footholds into a coherent territorial empire. In Southeast Asia, France assembled Indochina—Cochinchina, Annam, Tonkin, and Cambodia, later joined by Laos—through a mix of military conquest, imposed protectorates, and treaty coercion, completing the process with the formal Union of French Indochina in 1887. In Africa, French forces pushed inland from coastal bases in Senegal and along the Algerian frontier, using columns of colonial troops (often recruited locally) to subdue kingdoms and chieftaincies across the western Sahel—Mali, Guinea, Ivory Coast, and beyond—while a parallel advance from Gabon and the Congo basin secured Central African territory. By the 1890s these campaigns had given France the second-largest colonial empire in the world, trailing only Britain's.

Consider the conquest of Tonkin (northern Vietnam) in the mid-1880s as a representative case. French merchants and missionaries had operated there for decades, but formal annexation followed a specific pattern repeated across the empire: a pretext (attacks on French nationals, treaty violations, or "disorder" threatening trade) justified military intervention; a small, technologically superior expeditionary force defeated local rulers and Chinese forces backing them, as in the Sino-French War (1884–85); and victory was locked in through the Treaty of Tientsin, converting Tonkin into a French protectorate administered through remaining local authorities under a French resident-general.

Applying this pattern helps explain the pace of expansion elsewhere. When examining any French colonial acquisition in this era, ask three questions: What commercial or missionary presence preceded conquest? What military or diplomatic pretext triggered formal annexation? And what administrative form followed—direct rule, protectorate, or a mixed system? This framework distinguishes France's approach from Britain's more commerce-driven model and clarifies why French Africa and Indochina, despite covering vastly different geographies, share administrative structures traceable to the same Third Republic strategy of using colonies to project great-power status while extracting resources and recruiting labor and soldiers for future European conflicts.
