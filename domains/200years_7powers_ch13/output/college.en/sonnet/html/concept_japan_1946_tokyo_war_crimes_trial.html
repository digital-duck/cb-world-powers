Japan 1946 Tokyo War Crimes Trial

When the Allies occupied Japan in September 1945, they faced the same problem the Nuremberg Trials had just confronted in Europe: how do you hold individual leaders legally responsible for a war, rather than punishing an entire nation collectively? The answer was the International Military Tribunal for the Far East (IMTFE), convened in Tokyo from April 1946 to November 1948. Judges from eleven Allied nations — including the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, China, and India — tried 28 senior Japanese military and civilian leaders on three categories of charges: crimes against peace (planning and waging aggressive war), conventional war crimes, and crimes against humanity. Over two and a half years the tribunal heard 419 witnesses and reviewed 4,336 pieces of evidence, producing one of the longest and most document-heavy trials in legal history.

The verdicts, delivered in November 1948, sentenced seven defendants — including former Prime Minister Hideki Tōjō — to death; they were hanged at Sugamo Prison on December 23, 1948. Sixteen received life imprisonment, two died before the trial concluded, and one was ruled mentally unfit to stand trial. Smaller tribunals held across Asia in the following years went on to convict more than 5,500 additional Japanese personnel for atrocities including the Nanjing and Manila massacres.

The trial's limits are as instructive as its verdicts. Emperor Hirohito, the nominal commander-in-chief under whose authority the war was waged, was never charged — a decision General Douglas MacArthur made deliberately, calculating that prosecuting the emperor risked mass unrest and would undermine the stability needed to govern occupied Japan. Similarly, scientists and officers of Unit 731, which had conducted lethal human experimentation in China, received immunity in exchange for handing their research data to U.S. occupation authorities. Both choices illustrate a recurring tension in transitional justice: prosecutors must weigh strict accountability against the practical goal of building a stable postwar order, and the Tokyo Trial resolved that tension by holding the visible chain of command accountable while leaving the apex — and some of its most compromised technical specialists — untouched. Comparing this outcome to Nuremberg, where no equivalent figure was shielded, is a useful exercise in evaluating how occupation politics, not just legal principle, shapes the reach of a war-crimes tribunal.
