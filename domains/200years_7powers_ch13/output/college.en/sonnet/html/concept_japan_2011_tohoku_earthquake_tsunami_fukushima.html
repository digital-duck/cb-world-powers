Japan 2011 Tohoku Earthquake Tsunami Fukushima

Definition. On March 11, 2011, a magnitude 9.0 undersea earthquake struck off Japan's northeastern (Tōhoku) coast — the most powerful ever recorded in Japan and the fourth-most-powerful in modern global record-keeping. The rupture, along a subduction zone where the Pacific Plate slides beneath the Okhotsk Plate, displaced the seafloor and generated a tsunami with waves reaching over 40 meters in some coastal inlets. The wave overran seawalls designed for smaller events, killed roughly 18,000 people, and destroyed over 120,000 buildings. At the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant, the tsunami disabled backup diesel generators that cooled the reactors after the earthquake automatically shut them down, causing three reactor cores to overheat and partially melt down — the worst nuclear accident since Chernobyl in 1986.

Worked example. The disaster is best understood as a cascading-failure chain, not a single event. First link: the earthquake itself caused limited direct damage to Fukushima's reactors, which shut down safely as designed. Second link: the tsunami — arriving roughly 40 minutes later, higher than the plant's 5.7-meter seawall — flooded the emergency generators housed in basements, cutting power needed to circulate cooling water. Third link: without cooling, fuel rods in Units 1–3 overheated, reacted with steam to produce hydrogen gas, and triggered explosions that breached containment buildings, releasing radioactive cesium-137 and iodine-131. Some 154,000 residents were evacuated from a 20-km exclusion zone, many permanently. This chain illustrates a recurring lesson in disaster analysis: infrastructure failures often stem not from the initial hazard but from an underestimated secondary hazard (here, tsunami height exceeding the design assumption).

Problem-solving application. The disaster forced a direct policy reversal: pre-2011, nuclear power supplied about 30% of Japan's electricity, with plans to expand it to 50%. All 54 reactors were idled for safety review; by 2015 only a handful had restarted, and as of the early 2020s nuclear supplied under 10% of national generation, with Japan importing far more liquefied natural gas and coal to fill the gap — raising both costs and emissions. The case is frequently used in engineering-ethics and public-policy courses to examine a core question: how should societies set safety margins for low-probability, high-consequence events, given that the seawall's design had used a tsunami risk estimate later shown to be too optimistic?
