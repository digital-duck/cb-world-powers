Japan 1960 Economic Miracle

Building on its 1956 admission to the United Nations, which restored Japan's standing as a full participant in the international order, Japan spent the following three decades converting political rehabilitation into economic dominance. Between roughly 1960 and 1989, Japan's real GDP grew at an average annual rate above 8% through the 1960s and remained strong into the 1970s and 1980s, propelling the country from postwar reconstruction to the world's second-largest economy, behind only the United States.

This growth rested on a distinctive institutional model rather than any single policy. The Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI) coordinated "industrial policy," steering capital, licenses, and protection toward strategically chosen sectors — first steel and shipbuilding, then automobiles and consumer electronics, later semiconductors. Japanese firms organized into keiretsu, networks of companies linked by cross-shareholdings and a "main bank," which reduced the cost of capital and enabled long-term investment horizons unavailable to firms dependent on quarterly shareholder returns. Labor relations featured lifetime employment and seniority-based wages at large firms, which encouraged workers to invest in firm-specific skills and gave companies a stable workforce for retraining as production methods changed.

The manufacturing philosophy driving this growth is best illustrated by the Toyota Production System, which introduced kaizen (continuous incremental improvement) and just-in-time inventory management. Rather than stockpiling parts, factories received components only as needed, which cut waste and forced defects to be caught immediately rather than buried in inventory. Applied at scale, this let Japanese automakers undercut American and European competitors on cost while matching or exceeding them on reliability — a competitive advantage that reshaped global manufacturing practice far beyond Japan.

Consider a policy-analysis problem: a government official today points to Japan's model and proposes replicating MITI-style industrial policy to build a domestic semiconductor industry. Evaluating this requires understanding what actually made the model work — coordinated finance, patient capital, and workforce stability — and what later undermined it, since the same institutions (asset-inflated bank lending, cross-shareholding rigidity) contributed to the 1990s "Lost Decade" once the growth phase ended. A sound policy proposal must specify which mechanism is being borrowed and address the conditions, such as accountable bank oversight, needed to avoid the failure mode Japan itself later experienced.
