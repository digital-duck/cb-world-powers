The Munich Agreement (1938) rested on a bet: that ceding the Sudetenland would satisfy Hitler and preserve peace. That bet failed. In March 1939 Germany occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia, violating the agreement outright. Britain responded by extending a formal guarantee of Poland's independence — a public commitment that if Germany attacked Poland, Britain would declare war. This was the direct policy reversal that ended appeasement: deterrence replaced concession as the operating strategy.

Worked example. On September 1, 1939, Germany invaded Poland. Britain issued an ultimatum demanding withdrawal; Germany did not comply. On September 3, 1939, Britain declared war, honoring its guarantee. France declared war the same day. This sequence illustrates the mechanics of an alliance guarantee: it converts a bilateral conflict (Germany vs. Poland) into a multilateral one by pre-committing third parties to intervene. The guarantee only functions as deterrence if the threatened party believes it will be enforced; Hitler, having watched Britain and France concede at Munich, apparently judged the Polish guarantee would not be honored. He was wrong, and the underestimation of British resolve was itself a consequence of the credibility Britain had spent at Munich.

Problem-solving application. The following years show why a guarantee alone does not equal military capability. After Poland fell within weeks, Germany turned west in 1940, defeating France by June. Britain now stood alone in Western Europe against Nazi Germany, without a continental ally, fighting the Battle of Britain to prevent invasion and later waging the Battle of the Atlantic to keep supply lines open. Only with Germany's invasion of the USSR (June 1941) and Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor, bringing the United States into the war (December 1941), did Britain gain the industrial and manpower partners needed to shift from survival to eventual offensive victory. To analyze this period, distinguish three separate questions: what obligation triggered entry into the war (the Polish guarantee), what circumstance created strategic isolation (France's fall), and what external events ended that isolation (USSR and US entry) — each governed by different actors and incentives, and conflating them obscures why Britain's 1940–41 position was so precarious.
