In May 1940, as German forces swept through the Low Countries and France with devastating speed, the British government under Neville Chamberlain collapsed politically. Chamberlain, discredited by his earlier policy of appeasement and by the failure of the Norwegian campaign, resigned on May 10, 1940 — the very day Germany launched its invasion of France and the Low Countries. Winston Churchill, then First Lord of the Admiralty, became Prime Minister, forming a coalition government drawing from all major parties. His task was immediate and dire: the British Expeditionary Force, along with French and Belgian units, was being pushed back to the French coast at Dunkirk, facing encirclement and destruction.

Worked example — Dunkirk as strategic problem-solving. Between May 26 and June 4, 1940, Britain organized Operation Dynamo, an emergency evacuation combining Royal Navy destroyers with hundreds of civilian "little ships" — fishing boats, pleasure craft, and ferries — to pull soldiers off the beaches under sustained air attack. Roughly 338,000 troops were rescued, far exceeding initial estimates of 30,000–45,000. This was not a victory in military terms — nearly all heavy equipment was abandoned — but it preserved the trained manpower Britain needed to continue the war. Churchill's own assessment, delivered in his "we shall fight on the beaches" speech, reframed the evacuation as a foundation for resistance rather than a defeat, illustrating how political leadership can convert a logistical crisis into sustained morale and strategic capacity.

Application — the Battle of Britain as a systems test. Germany's plan for invasion (Operation Sea Lion) depended on first achieving air superiority over the English Channel. From July through October 1940, the Royal Air Force, using the integrated Dowding System — radar early warning, ground-based fighter control, and coordinated squadron response — inflicted unsustainable losses on the Luftwaffe. This was fundamentally a resource-allocation and information-processing problem: RAF Fighter Command could direct scarce aircraft precisely because radar data reduced uncertainty about enemy position and timing, an early demonstration of how information systems multiply the effectiveness of limited physical resources. Germany's failure to achieve air superiority forced an indefinite postponement of invasion, marking the first major strategic setback for Nazi Germany and validating Churchill's decision to fight on rather than negotiate.
