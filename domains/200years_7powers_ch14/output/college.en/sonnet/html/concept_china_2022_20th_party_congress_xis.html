China 2022 20Th Party Congress Xis

At the 20th National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party in October 2022, Xi Jinping was reappointed General Secretary for a third five-year term, breaking a norm — not a formal constitutional term limit — that had governed the party's top leadership since Deng Xiaoping's reforms. Deng, having lived through the catastrophe of Mao Zedong's unchecked, decades-long rule, engineered a system of collective leadership and orderly succession: general secretaries would serve two terms, transfer power at a fixed retirement age, and groom a successor in advance. Jiang Zemin and Hu Jintao both followed this pattern, stepping down after two terms in 2002 and 2012 respectively. Xi's third term, following the 2018 removal of the separate two-term limit on the state presidency from China's constitution, signaled the effective end of that norm.

The Congress also revealed the extent of Xi's consolidation. The new 24-member Politburo and 7-member Politburo Standing Committee were filled almost entirely with Xi loyalists — including Li Qiang, promoted to Premier despite having no prior State Council experience, a break from precedent that normally required such experience. Premier Li Keqiang and other reform-oriented, non-Xi-aligned officials were removed from the leadership entirely rather than retiring gracefully, and in a widely circulated moment captured on camera, former leader Hu Jintao was visibly escorted out of the closing session, underscoring the sidelining of rival factions.

Analyzing causation: why did this consolidation succeed where it might once have been resisted? Three converging factors mattered. First, the "zero-COVID" leadership crisis of 2020–2022 had, paradoxically, been used to justify centralized emergency authority rather than undermine Xi, even as economic costs mounted. Second, an anti-corruption campaign begun in 2012 had already removed thousands of potential rival officials over the preceding decade, narrowing the field of credible challengers. Third, no institutional check — an independent legislature, judiciary, or free press — existed to contest the norm's removal.

Problem-solving application: to assess an authoritarian succession norm's durability, examine three questions: (1) Is the limit codified in law or merely conventional? (2) Do independent institutions enforce it? (3) Has the incumbent removed rivals in advance? Applying this framework to 2018–2022 China explains why the two-term norm broke while formally similar constraints elsewhere have held.
