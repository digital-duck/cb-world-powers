China 2022 End Zero Covid Property Crisis

By late 2022, two of China's structural pressures converged into an acute crisis. First, the "Zero-COVID" policy — mandatory mass testing, snap lockdowns, and centralized quarantine — had held case counts low for nearly three years but at rising economic and social cost. In November 2022, a deadly apartment fire in Urumqi, where residents suspected lockdown measures obstructed rescue, triggered protests across major cities, including rare public calls for political liberalization. Within weeks, the government abruptly dismantled Zero-COVID, lifting most restrictions without a phased transition or adequate vaccination buffer among the elderly.

Second, and simultaneously, the property sector — long China's largest driver of GDP growth and household wealth (real estate and related industries account for roughly a quarter of GDP) — was unraveling. Developers had financed rapid construction through heavy debt, a strategy regulators tried to curb in 2020 with the "three red lines" policy limiting leverage. Evergrande, China's second-largest developer, defaulted on international bonds in late 2021, and the strain spread through the sector into 2022–2023, freezing pre-sold but unfinished housing projects and shaking confidence in an asset class where most urban Chinese households hold the bulk of their savings.

Analytical application: treat this as a case study in policy sequencing and systemic risk. Ask: why did lifting Zero-COVID and the property collapse reinforce each other rather than occurring independently? Consider the transmission channels: local governments, dependent on land sales to developers for revenue, faced simultaneous fiscal strain from three years of pandemic spending and a collapsing land market — reducing their capacity to cushion the reopening shock (hospital capacity, subsidies). Meanwhile, reopening did not restore consumer confidence as expected, because households whose primary asset (housing) was impaired became more likely to save than spend, dampening the "revenge spending" recovery many economists forecast.

A useful framework here is contagion mapping: draw the linkages between (1) household balance sheets, (2) local government revenue, (3) developer solvency, and (4) aggregate demand, then trace how a shock in one node — Evergrande's default — propagates through the others. This exercise, standard in analyzing financial crises (compare to the 2008 U.S. mortgage crisis), shows why "decoupled" reforms in an authoritarian system with tight state-economy linkages rarely stay decoupled, and helps explain why China's 2023 recovery underperformed reopening-driven expectations.
